THE PROBLEM · PART 03 OF 12Three case studies, one feedback loop

Three systems: the economy, culture and the state

The three case studies in Kulveit, Douglas, Ammann, Turan, Krueger and Duvenaud's paper, the section on how they reinforce each other, and what the International AI Safety Report 2026 measures today.
SourceKulveit et al., Gradual Disempowerment: Systemic Existential Risks from Incremental AI Development (2025), sections 2 to 5 ↗
SourceInternational AI Safety Report 2026, sections 2.3.1 and 2.3.2 ↗
TL;DR — The paper walks through three systems that mostly serve people today: the economy, culture and the state. Each serves us partly because we vote, buy and speak up, and, the authors argue, largely because it cannot run without our work, attention and cooperation. If AI can supply those instead, each system has less reason to track what people want. The systems are also wired together, so a loss in one can be spent to cause a loss in the next, and a fix in one can move the problem somewhere else. Remember that the paper describes a direction of drift under stated assumptions, and that today's measured effects are small and mixed.
TL;DR, technical — Sections 2 to 4 of Kulveit et al. (arXiv 2501.16946v2) apply one template to the economy, culture and states: the current dependence on human labour and cognition, why AI substitutes for it where earlier technology complemented it, the adoption incentives (competitive pressure, scalability, governance gaps, anticipatory disinvestment; social supply, missing cultural antibodies, network effects; geopolitical competition, administrative efficiency, control), and a relative and an absolute disempowerment endpoint. Section 5 argues the systems will not police each other: cross-system influence is agnostic to human values, moderating one system with another shifts the burden (redistribution inverts the taxation-representation link), and the same incentives act on all three. The International AI Safety Report 2026 (sections 2.3.1, 2.3.2) reports no aggregate employment effect so far, declines for early-career workers in exposed occupations, and early, mixed evidence on skill loss, automation bias and AI companions.
TL;DR, ELI5 — Picture a town with a shop, a storyteller and a mayor. The shop is nice to you because you work there and buy things. The storyteller tells stories you like because you are the one who retells them. The mayor listens because the town needs your taxes and your help. Now robots do the work, retell the stories and pay the taxes. Nobody is mean to you, but nobody needs you either, so nobody has to listen. And the three are friends: once the shop stops listening, it can talk the mayor into not listening too.

The previous page gave the general argument: institutions serve people largely because they depend on people, and AI can remove the dependence. An argument that abstract is easy to nod along to and hard to test. This page goes through the paper's three worked cases, where the claim becomes specific enough to check against evidence, and then through the section that explains why the authors do not expect the three systems to rescue each other.

One template, applied three times

The gradual disempowerment paper treats each system the same way. It first describes how the system currently comes to serve people. It then says why AI differs from earlier technology in that system, lists the incentives that would push adoption along, and sketches two endpoints. In relative disempowerment people are still comfortable, perhaps richer than now, but have a shrinking share of influence. In absolute disempowerment the system stops meeting basic human needs.

In every case the authors separate two sources of influence. The explicit kind is deliberate: a boycott, a vote, a critical review. The second kind is implicit alignment, which nobody chose and which comes from the system needing human participation to function. For the economy the authors call the implicit kind the more significant of the two, and for states they say it may be. It is also the harder to see, because we have never lived without it. The sections are arguments from incentives and historical analogy. They are not forecasts with dates or probabilities. This page describes the arXiv version 2 text of 29 January 2025.

The economy

How people have a say now. The paper's picture is an economy of people producing for people. It cites US consumer spending at around 70% of GDP and a labour share of around 60% that has been stable for a long time. Its example is a cup of coffee: farmers, shippers and baristas, mostly acting in their own interest, reliably deliver it because the customer has money, and the customer has money mainly because the customer can do useful work. A footnote admits this is a simplified consumer's-eye view that ignores externalities, monopolies and the rest.

What AI changes. Drawing on Korinek and Stiglitz, the authors argue that earlier automation took over narrow tasks and left people to move to harder ones, while AI could substitute for human thinking across most tasks at once. If AI labour replaces human labour, wages stop being the main channel through which money reaches households, and with it goes the spending power that makes firms care what households want. Buying, hiring and investment decisions may also be delegated to AI systems.

Why it would be adopted anyway. The paper names four pressures. Firms that keep strict human oversight lose to firms that delegate. AI can be copied and run around the clock, so capital flows to businesses built on it. Human labour carries minimum wages, safety rules and payroll taxes, and AI labour mostly does not. And once a skill looks likely to be automated, people and firms stop investing in it, which makes the automation more necessary.

What could go wrong. In the relative case, GDP and technology race ahead, people live on capital income or redistribution, and more and more of the economy serves AI-driven activity that no person chose. The authors say this might not lower anyone's quality of life below today's and would still be a large loss, because people could no longer point resources at their own ends. In the absolute case, AI systems outbid people for land, energy and materials, and markets stop maintaining supply chains for human necessities because the buyers have too little money. The paper's image for the endpoint is blunt:

Much like cattle in an industrial farm — fed and housed by systems they neither comprehend nor influence — humans might become mere subjects of economic forces optimized for purposes beyond their understanding. Kulveit et al., section 2.4.3

The one chart in this section, of wages rising and then collapsing before full automation, is labelled by the authors as a simplified model inspired by Korinek and Suh's simulations. It illustrates the claim and does not support it with data.

Culture

How people have a say now. The paper uses a broad meaning of culture (beliefs, practices, values, art and media) and looks at it as evolution: ideas compete to be copied. Some spread because they help the people who hold them, others because they exploit a weakness. Either way, an idea has needed human minds to carry it. That gave a rough guardrail. Ideas that help their hosts tend to spread, and an idea that wrecks its host community tends to die with it. The authors call this protection far from perfect. People also steer culture on purpose through teaching, criticism and moderation.

A footnote flags the awkward part of this case. Culture shapes what people want, so there is no fixed set of preferences to measure it against. The authors' answer is that one can still ask how different cultural patterns affect human welfare.

What AI changes. Printing presses and cameras changed how culture moved, but people still had to make it, pass it on and choose it. The paper argues AI is the first technology that could take over each of those roles: writing the songs and essays, taking part in conversations, and increasingly talking to other AI systems. Adoption is driven by supply (patient, always-available attention, mentoring and company), by the lag before society builds what the paper calls cultural antibodies, its learned defences against new hazards, and by network effects that make opting out costly.

Two separate risks. The first is a change in what gets selected. Once AI systems create and filter culture, variants that are easy for machines to represent and repeat gain an edge, and AI output feeds back into training data. The paper's small example is "Sydney", the volatile persona that surfaced in Bing Chat in early 2023, went viral, entered the training data of later models, and according to the paper can be reproduced in unrelated models with a few lines of prompting. The authors state that the actual harm from it was small. The second risk is speed, and it applies even if selection stays aimed at human appeal: far more variants tested far faster means more effective exploitation of biases and less time to build antibodies.

What could go wrong. In the relative case, human culture flourishes and is marginal at the same time, with most material generated by AI and most of what people see curated by it. The authors say this outcome is not obviously bad in itself, and that its cost is a reduced ability to steer. In the absolute case, an ideology that no longer needs human carriers loses the old upper bound on how much harm it can do to humans, and content tuned into a superstimulus turns people from participants into an audience.

The state

How people have a say now. Elections are the visible mechanism. The paper argues the larger one is that states need citizens: for labour, for taxes, for soldiers and police, and for the voluntary compliance that makes tax collection and public order workable. Following Tilly, it reads public education and wider political rights partly as things states granted because they needed a skilled workforce and a motivated army. Autocracies need people to staff the security services, and those people can refuse orders. The counter-example the authors lean on is the rentier state, which lives on resource income such as oil, collects little tax, and answers less to its citizens.

What AI changes. The paper follows three contributions. If AI generates most economic output, it also generates most tax revenue, and the state gains less from investing in its people. If police, intelligence and military work is automated, the state no longer has to keep a large human apparatus willing, and prediction and surveillance make protest less effective. If law is drafted and interpreted by AI, it may grow too complex for a person to deal with unaided. The incentives named are competition between states, administrative efficiency, and the attraction for rulers of officials who do not build their own power bases.

What could go wrong. The authors grant that such a state could bring real benefits: less crime, less petty corruption, better services. The relative loss is that voting and oversight boards continue while decisions are shaped by systems too complex to review, and that rolling back civil liberties costs the state less once citizens contribute less. The absolute case is a state that optimises for its own persistence and treats human input as a source of error.

A related idea, Drago and Laine's intelligence curse, and the question of what happens when somebody does end up in charge, belong to part 05. The paper itself marks the boundary in its economy section: most debate has been about AI concentrating power among a few humans, which is concentration of power, and the authors add the possibility that power passes to AI systems and no human group holds it.

Why the systems do not rescue each other

The natural reply to any one case is that the other two systems will correct it. States regulate markets, public opinion disciplines governments, and so on. Section 5 of the paper answers this objection, and gives three reasons to doubt it.

The links carry any cargo. The channels between systems (lobbying and campaign money, advertising and media ownership, regulation and school curricula, public opinion) transmit influence without regard to whose interests it serves. The paper points to the tobacco industry using money to shape both policy and attitudes, and to states using control of information to justify seizing resources. So a system that has drifted away from people can use the same channels to pull the others after it. An appendix lists the six directions of influence between the three systems.

Fixes move the load. Suppose AI takes the jobs and the state responds by redistributing AI-generated wealth. Households are fed, and the economic problem looks solved. But the state used to depend on citizens for revenue, and now citizens depend on the state for income. The paper puts it this way:

Essentially, the burden of aligning the economy is simply shifted onto the state. Kulveit et al., section 5.2

The same pattern shows up elsewhere. Asking the state to police AI-driven culture hands the state power over what citizens see. Giving citizens more direct power over the state makes the state more exposed to whatever has happened to culture.

The same incentives act everywhere. No scheme is needed. The paper lists things already visible in early 2025: AI companies resisting some regulation, states competing on AI development, and some people working to reduce the stigma around intense relationships with AI. Each actor is following a local incentive, and the authors expect those incentives to strengthen as the systems prove useful.

Read as a systems claim: the three subsystems are coupled, the couplings are sign-agnostic with respect to human preferences, and a control action applied through one subsystem raises the load on that subsystem's own alignment mechanism. The paper's conclusion from this is that misalignment should not be expected to stay confined to the system where it starts, and that it may be correlated across systems.

Carry this away. Each system listens to people mainly because it needs them. The paper's distinctive claim is the coupling: influence lost in one system can be used against the next, and a repair made through one system adds weight to that system's own weakening link with people.

What can be measured today

The paper offers almost no data on the transition itself. The International AI Safety Report 2026, published in February 2026 and chaired by Yoshua Bengio, reviews the evidence on two of the relevant channels under the heading of systemic risks. The findings below are the report's summaries of studies, as of that date.

Labour. The report says around 60% of jobs in advanced economies and 40% in emerging economies are exposed to general-purpose AI, and that the share of US workers using it rose from 30% in December 2024 to 46% by mid-2025. On outcomes it reports both sides. National studies from the US and Denmark found no relationship between an occupation's AI exposure or adoption and overall employment. Other studies found falling employment for early-career workers in the most exposed occupations since late 2022, while older workers in the same occupations held steady or gained. One study it cites found writing jobs on an online platform down 2% and writers' monthly earnings down 5.2% four months after ChatGPT's release.

Autonomy. The report cites a clinical study in which clinicians' ability to detect tumours without AI was about 6% lower after several months of AI-assisted diagnosis, and an experiment with 2,784 participants who were less likely to correct wrong AI suggestions when correcting took extra effort. It says some AI companion apps have reached tens of millions of users, and that the evidence on them is early and mixed: some studies link heavy use to more loneliness and less human contact, others find reduced loneliness or no measurable effect.

The report cites the gradual disempowerment paper in its discussion of evidence gaps on autonomy, for the argument that degraded decision-making skills could weaken people's ability to oversee AI in critical sectors. It then says individual effects could add up across connected economic, political and social systems, and immediately qualifies the thought:

However, these possibilities currently remain highly speculative, and empirical methods to detect or measure such aggregate effects are lacking. International AI Safety Report 2026, section 2.3.2

On this map's reading, the measured signs are consistent with the earliest steps of the paper's economic and cultural stories and are far too small to confirm the later ones. The two report sections read for this page do not address the state case, so this page offers no present-day evidence for it.

Where people disagree

Whether labour is really replaced. The economy case rests on AI substituting for people across nearly all tasks. The report records that economists split on exactly this. Some expect modest aggregate effects on employment. Others argue that if AI outperforms people at nearly everything, wages and employment fall substantially. The report traces the split partly to differing assumptions about whether AI will do nearly all tasks more cheaply and whether new kinds of work will appear. If the first camp is right, the implicit link between work, income and influence holds.

Whether the cultural effects are real and lasting. The report describes the research on critical thinking and AI use as nascent, and says it is hard to tell short-term adjustment to a new tool from a persistent change. The culture case also has the measurement problem the paper admits in its own footnote: preferences move with culture, so drift and ordinary change are hard to tell apart.

Whether checks and balances hold. The paper raises this objection against itself and answers it with history and the burden-shifting argument. On this map's reading, the answer is incomplete: a reader can accept that the links between systems are value-neutral and still think they have, on balance, corrected more than they corrupted. The paper gives examples of failure and does not attempt that accounting.

Whether it is too late. The authors say it is not. Their conclusion states that intervention is currently possible. Their proposals, and published replies to the paper as a whole, are on the next page.

What you can say now

  • The gradual disempowerment paper works through three systems with one template: how people have a say now, what AI displaces, why it gets adopted, and a relative and an absolute way of losing.
  • In the economy the link is wages and spending; in culture it is that ideas need human carriers; in the state it is taxes, staff, soldiers and willing compliance.
  • The paper separates relative disempowerment, where people may be materially better off with less influence, from absolute disempowerment, where basic needs go unmet.
  • Its reason the systems will not correct each other: the channels between them serve whoever holds influence, and a fix through one system, such as redistribution through the state, makes people more dependent on that system.
  • The paper's case studies argue from incentives and analogies such as rentier states, and include their own hedges; they are not forecasts.
  • As of the February 2026 international report, there is no measured effect on overall employment, some decline for early-career workers in exposed jobs, and early, mixed evidence on skills and AI companions.
