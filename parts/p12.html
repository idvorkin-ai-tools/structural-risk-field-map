International coordination

What governments have signed together on AI since 2023, what they have built, and the proposals for agreements with teeth. Read from the summit texts, the International AI Safety Report, the UN panel's pages, and two papers that disagree about deterrence.
SourceThe Bletchley Declaration by Countries Attending the AI Safety Summit (2023) ↗
SourceBengio et al., International AI Safety Report 2026 ↗
TL;DR — If every country fears falling behind, no country can afford to slow down alone, so the fix has to be agreed between them. Since 2023 governments have signed four summit statements, set up two science panels and linked their testing institutes. None of it binds anyone. The proposals that would bind (agreed red lines, a treaty with inspections) all depend on one hard thing: each side being able to check what the other is doing.
TL;DR, technical — The state-level instruments so far are declaratory: Bletchley (2023), the Seoul declaration and ministerial statement (2024), the Paris statement (2025) and the New Delhi declaration (2026), with signatory sets that differ each time. The shared-evidence layer is the International AI Safety Report (UK-commissioned, no policy recommendations) and the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI (A/RES/79/325, non-prescriptive). Binding proposals split on verification: Katzke and Futerman model superintelligence development as a trust dilemma where imperfect verification suffices; Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang call unverified treaties toothless and propose deterrence by sabotage (MAIM).
TL;DR, ELI5 — Imagine kids racing bikes down a steep hill. Each one would brake if the others did, but nobody wants to brake first. So far the kids have met four times. The first time they agreed that crashing is bad. They have not agreed on a rule, and nobody is standing at the bottom checking who braked.

Every other lever in this arc is pulled by one government inside its own borders. The problem this map describes does not stop at borders: if one country slows its labs, the work can move, and the country that slowed pays the cost alone. This last page asks what states have done together, from the documents they signed, and what the serious proposals for doing more would need.

As of November 2026. In force: no binding international agreement that limits frontier AI development was found in any primary source read for this page. Signed but voluntary: the Bletchley, Seoul, Paris and New Delhi texts. Standing bodies: the International AI Safety Report (second edition, February 2026), the UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI (members appointed February 2026) and the institute network, now renamed. Proposed: the Global Call for AI Red Lines asks for an agreement by the end of 2026; this page found no primary record of one as of 23 November. Renamed: the UK institute (February 2025) and the US institute (June 2025, and again on NIST's site after September 2026).

Why this lever is different

Part 6 described race dynamics: each competitor trims caution because it expects the others to. A national rule can stop that among firms in one country. It cannot stop it between countries, because no government sits above them. The 2026 International AI Safety Report puts the state-level version plainly: countries may see a trade-off between building domestic capability and adopting safety measures that slow it, especially if they think others are not adopting comparable ones.

The same report lists what goes wrong without coordination: harms that land outside the country where the model was built, firms relocating to the lightest regime, and national standards that do not fit together. Coordination is the lever aimed at those gaps. Measured by what anyone is obliged to do, it is also the weakest lever in this arc.

Four summits, four texts

The summit series began at Bletchley Park in November 2023. Each host published a text.

Two things stand out. First, the list of names changes every time. The two countries where most frontier models are built appear together on the first text and the fourth, and not on the two in between. The official pages do not say why a country is absent, so neither does this page.

Second, the subject moved. Bletchley is a document about danger at the frontier:

Many risks arising from AI are inherently international in nature, and so are best addressed through international cooperation. Bletchley Declaration, 2023

The New Delhi text, which has the longest list of endorsers, is a document about spreading the benefits. In the copy converted for this page the words "safety" and "risk" do not appear at all. On this map's reading, the series bought breadth by asking for less.

Seoul also produced the company-side pledges, where developers promised to publish a frontier safety framework. Those are promises by firms, not states, and part 10 covers them.

A shared evidence base

The summits did leave one durable product. The countries at Bletchley asked for an independent scientific report, and it now exists as the International AI Safety Report. Its own site says it is commissioned by the UK government, chaired by Yoshua Bengio, written by more than 100 independent experts, and supported by a secretariat inside the UK's institute. An interim version came out before Seoul, the first full edition in January 2025 before Paris, and the second in February 2026 before New Delhi.

Two design choices matter for this page. The report says it does not make specific policy recommendations; its job is to state what the evidence shows. And an Expert Advisory Panel of government nominees gives technical feedback only, while the writers keep full discretion over content. Rivals can both cite a document built that way. The cost is that it tells nobody what to do. The panel list as fetched for this page has rows for China, the EU, the UK and the UN, and no row for the United States.

The United Nations has built a parallel body. The Independent International Scientific Panel on AI was established by General Assembly resolution A/RES/79/325 on 26 August 2025. Its FAQ says the Assembly appointed 40 members in February 2026, that they elected Yoshua Bengio and Maria Ressa as inaugural co-chairs (the FAQ now lists Martha Palmer and Vukosi Marivate as their successors, elected in November 2026), and that a preliminary report followed on 1 July 2026. The same resolution created a Global Dialogue on AI Governance, open to all member states, which was launched in New York on 25 September 2025 and held its first annual session in Geneva on 6–7 July 2026. The UN is explicit that neither is a regulator: the panel will not set rules or prescribe policy, and the dialogue is not an enforcement body.

So there are two international science bodies, and both stop at evidence.

The institutes, and what their new names say

After Bletchley, several governments set up an AI safety institute: a public team that tests frontier models. In November 2024 the United States convened an International Network of AI Safety Institutes in San Francisco with ten founding members: Australia, Canada, the EU, France, Japan, Kenya, Korea, Singapore, the UK and the US. China is not on that list.

Then the names changed.

  • United Kingdom. On 14 February 2025 the government renamed its body the AI Security Institute. The announcement says it will concentrate on risks with security implications, such as weapons, cyber-attacks and crime.
  • United States. A note on NIST's news pages says the US AI Safety Institute was re-established in June 2025 as the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI). As of this page's check the NIST page is titled Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI). NIST attributes the wording to Executive Order 14434 of 29 September 2026, which tells agencies to use the term “super intelligence” and does not itself name the centre. The centre's listed tasks include representing US interests abroad against foreign regulation it considers burdensome.
  • The network. The UK institute's July 2026 post calls it the International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation and Science, with the same ten members, and describes its work as the science of evaluating models.

The word "safety" has gone from all three names. What remains is useful: shared test methods, joint testing exercises, a best-practice document for evaluators. It is cooperation on how to measure. It is not an agreement on what to do when a measurement comes back bad.

Proposals for limits that bind

The loudest request for more is the Global Call for AI Red Lines, launched during the UN General Assembly session that opened in September 2025. Its site counts more than 300 prominent signatories, including 15 Nobel and Turing laureates and 11 former heads of state and ministers, plus more than 90 organisations. The ask is one sentence:

We urge governments to reach an international agreement on red lines for AI — ensuring they are operational, with robust enforcement mechanisms — by the end of 2026. Global Call for AI Red Lines

A red line here means a prohibition on a use or a behaviour, agreed between states. The campaign does not endorse a list. Its FAQ gives examples, such as no AI in nuclear launch decisions and no systems that copy or improve themselves without human authorisation, and suggests an independent body modelled on the International Atomic Energy Agency to check compliance. It is a petition by scientists and former officials, not a commitment by any government. The organisers' own September 2026 letter says the missing piece is a political decision by governments to negotiate.

The fullest argument that such a decision is rational comes from Corin Katzke and Gideon Futerman in "The Manhattan Trap". Part 6 covers why they think a national race defeats itself; their claim about agreements belongs here. They argue the situation is a trust dilemma and not a prisoner's dilemma: each state would prefer that nobody builds the dangerous thing, and would only build it if it believed a rival was doing so. In that kind of game, they argue, checking does not need to be perfect. It needs to be good enough that each side keeps believing the other is holding back.

In their illustrative payoff table mutual cooperation pays each side far more than mutual defection, and defecting against a cooperator pays less than cooperating with one. Both cooperate-cooperate and defect-defect are therefore Nash equilibria, unlike a prisoner's dilemma where defection dominates. The critical probability of the rival's cooperation below which a state defects falls as the gap between the two equilibria widens, which is why they claim imperfect verification can sustain the cooperative one.

Two neighbours both want a quiet street. Neither will buy a loud alarm unless they think the other already has. They do not need cameras in each other's houses. A glance over the fence now and then is enough to keep both calm.

They also argue that arms control for AI in general is unlikely to work, because military and civilian uses look the same, while control of the very largest training projects might, because those need huge data centres that are hard to hide. That leans on the logic of compute governance from part 7. Their own footnote concedes the weak point: if training can be spread out, or algorithms get much more efficient, the projects stop being visible.

Boaz Barak's essay, discussed in part 4, ends in the same place from a different direction: he lists international cooperation on pacing the rate of AI progress among the things a good outcome needs, and says he does not yet know how it would be implemented.

The alternative: deterrence without a treaty

Dan Hendrycks, Eric Schmidt and Alexandr Wang, in "Superintelligence Strategy", doubt the treaty route. They argue that a moratorium without verification would be toothless, because each side would fear the other's secret work and carry on. Their alternative is MAIM, mutual assured AI malfunction: any state making a bid for dominance should expect rivals to sabotage the project, and that expectation restrains everyone without lengthy negotiation. They do not discard verification. They present it as something states would adopt during the stand-off to lower the risk of sabotage or pre-emptive attack. Part 6 has the proposal and its critics.

Why coordination is hard

Checking. Every binding proposal above rests on verification, and the two papers disagree on whether today's tools are enough. Inspection that reveals too much is its own problem. Katzke and Futerman borrow the idea of a transparency-security trade-off: a state must show enough to prove it is complying, but not so much that it exposes military secrets.

The race. The incentive that makes coordination necessary also makes it hard to start: a country that believes its rival is not holding back will not hold back either.

The table. The widest forums (New Delhi, the UN dialogue) say the least about limits. The forums that do technical work (the institute network) do not include China. Of the four summit texts, only Bletchley combines both major powers with language about catastrophic harm.

The opposite lever. An export control is what a state does when it is not coordinating with a rival: it acts alone, or with allies, to restrict what the rival can buy. Part 8 covers it. On this map's reading the two levers pull against each other, and none of the sources read here resolves that.

Carry this away. States have agreed on what to study and how to measure. They have not agreed on what to stop. Every proposal to close that gap comes down to whether one country can check another's largest AI projects well enough to trust it.

Where people disagree

Is a treaty realistic? Katzke and Futerman say yes: the danger is mutual, so restraint is in each state's own interest, and they conclude the larger obstacle is will:

Political will rather than technical means is the more important barrier to ASI development control. Katzke and Futerman, "The Manhattan Trap"

Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang say militaries want exactly the hazardous capabilities a treaty would ban, so reciprocal restraint is implausible without the threat of force behind it.

Is coordination even good? The International AI Safety Report gives the sceptical case without taking a side: common rules cost each country some freedom to choose and adapt its own, and reaching them can mean protracted negotiation between governments that want different things.

Is the current track progress or drift? One reading: New Delhi put the United States, China, Russia and the EU on one page again, and shared measurement science is a base that limits can be built on. The other, which the red-lines organisers take, is that statements and science panels are not limits. The documents support both.

What you can say now

  • As of November 2026 no primary source read for this page shows a binding international agreement limiting frontier AI development; the four summit texts are all voluntary.
  • The United States and China both appear on the Bletchley (2023) and New Delhi (2026) texts, and not together on the Seoul or Paris ones.
  • The International AI Safety Report and the UN scientific panel are both built to supply evidence and both decline to prescribe policy.
  • The UK and US testing bodies and their international network have all dropped "safety" from their names since early 2025.
  • The Global Call for AI Red Lines asks for a binding agreement by the end of 2026; it is a petition, not a government commitment.
