LEVERS · PART 08 OF 12The compute lever in use

Export controls

A dated record of United States controls on advanced chips, read from the rules the Bureau of Industry and Security published in the Federal Register, with Sastry et al. for the logic of the lever.
SourceBureau of Industry and Security, Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors, interim final rule, 91 FR 74970 (2026) ↗
SourceSastry, Heim, Belfield, Anderljung, Brundage et al., Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence (2024) ↗
TL;DR — Part 07 argued that chips are the one AI input a government can count and withhold. The United States has been doing that since October 2022, by requiring a licence to send its most capable AI chips, and the machines that make them, to certain countries and companies. The rules have been rewritten about once a year. Each rewrite chases a way around the last one: weaker chips bought in bulk, chips routed through a third country, and most recently chips that stay abroad and are rented over the network. The thing to remember is what the lever touches. It decides who can obtain hardware. It says nothing about how carefully anyone uses it.
TL;DR, technical — BIS created ECCNs 3A090 and 4A090 in October 2022 (87 FR 62186), re-based them on total processing performance and performance density in October 2023 and added a headquarters-based end-use control (88 FR 73458). The January 2025 diffusion rule (90 FR 4544) added a worldwide licence requirement, per-country TPP allocations and ECCN 4E091 for closed model weights trained with more than 1026 operations. BIS announced non-enforcement in May 2025 and removed its main provisions on 19 November 2026 (91 FR 74970), adding a data-centre end-use control at 12,000,000 aggregate TPP and a five-country licence requirement. A January 2026 rule (91 FR 1684) moved some chip exports to China from presumption of denial to case-by-case review.
TL;DR, ELI5 — One country's shops sell the biggest, fastest ovens. The government tells the shops: ask us before you sell a big oven to certain customers. So some customers buy lots of medium ovens, or get a friend in another town to buy one, or pay to bake in somebody else's kitchen. Each time, the government writes a new rule for that trick. The rules are about who gets an oven. They are not about what gets baked.

Parts 05 and 06 described two structural problems: power pooling in whoever controls the most capable systems, and competitors cutting caution to keep up. Part 07 introduced compute as a place where a government can push on both. This page looks at the compute lever that is already in use, the export control, and follows it through four years of rule changes. The aim is descriptive: what each rule says, what stands today, and what kind of thing this lever can and cannot move.

As of November 2026. In force: licence requirements for advanced computing chips going to a defined set of country groups, to the United Arab Emirates unless the recipient is an approved entity, and since 19 November 2026 to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Brazil and Thailand; a control on chips for data centres anywhere that serve large volumes of compute to companies headquartered in the most restricted destinations; and a case-by-case review path, opened in January 2026, for some chips exported from the United States to China or Macau. Withdrawn: the January 2025 diffusion framework, including its control on model weights, whose main provisions were removed from the regulations on 19 November 2026. Open: the November rule is an interim final rule that took effect on publication and accepts comments until 19 January 2027, so its text may still change.

What kind of rule this is

Every rule in the table below is a regulation, not a statute and not a proposal. The Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), part of the Department of Commerce, writes the Export Administration Regulations (EAR). The rules cite the Export Control Reform Act of 2018 as their authority, and several note that the Act lets BIS issue them without the usual notice-and-comment period. That is why most arrive as "interim final" rules: they bind first and take comments afterwards.

A control here rarely means a ban. It means a licence is required, and the rule sets a review posture: presumption of denial, presumption of approval, or case by case. Much of what changed between 2022 and 2026 is the posture and the list of places it applies to.

The rules sort destinations into lettered country groups. The ones that recur are D:1, D:4 and D:5, which the October 2023 rule describes as groups defined by national security concerns, missile technology concerns and United States arms embargoes respectively, and A:5 and A:6, whose members are excluded from the requirement. China is in D:5. Macau is named separately throughout.

The unit the rules count in is total processing performance. The 2023 rule defines TPP as 2 × MacTOPS × the bit length of the operation, summed over the processing units on the chip, where MacTOPS is the theoretical peak number of trillions of multiply-accumulate operations per second. Performance density is TPP divided by die area. ECCN 3A090.a covers chips with TPP of 4800 or more, or TPP of 1600 or more with a performance density of 5.92 or more. Part 09 covers the different unit, training operations, that laws about models use.

2022 and 2023: chips, then the ways around them

The October 2022 rule set the pattern. It did not try to regulate AI. It picked out a class of hardware by technical parameters, attached a licence requirement for one destination, and added controls on the equipment needed to make such hardware. BIS wrote that it intended to limit China's ability to obtain the manufacturing capability to produce advanced chips for uses contrary to United States interests.

A year later BIS published its response to what it had learned, and the changes are a catalogue of workarounds. One was buying chips that sat just under the line:

A performance density parameter prevents the workaround of simply purchasing a larger number of smaller datacenter AI chips which, if combined, would be equally powerful as restricted chips.
BIS, 88 FR 73458, section C

A second was geography. The 2023 rule widened the destination list because of concern about transshipment and diversion through other countries. A third was corporate structure: a company based in a restricted country could set up a data centre somewhere unrestricted. So the rule added a licence requirement that follows the headquarters of the entity the chips are for, wherever the chips physically go.

The fourth workaround the 2023 rule named but only partly addressed. A commenter on the 2022 rule had pointed out that renting compute as a service lets a workload run on chips that never cross a controlled border. BIS answered that it shared the concern and was still evaluating a regulatory response, and it asked cloud providers for comment. A dedicated rule came three years later.

January 2025: the diffusion framework, and its removal

The January 2025 rule was the widest version of the lever. Its premise, as BIS put it then, was that exporting advanced models or the means to produce them anywhere outside the United States carries some risk of theft or diversion. So it made the licence requirement for the top chip class worldwide and then built exceptions back in.

  • Nineteen listed destinations, a list that includes the United States itself, could generally keep getting the top chips without a licence if the recipient certified compliance with the rule's requirements.
  • D:5 destinations and Macau stayed under the existing strict rules.
  • Everyone else got a small-order exception (up to 26,900,000 TPP per recipient per calendar year), a cumulative per-country allocation of 790,000,000 TPP for 2025 through 2027, and a route to much larger volumes for data-centre operators who accepted verified security conditions.

The November 2026 rule refers to these groupings as "tiers". The 2025 text does not use the word.

The rule also did something the 2022 and 2023 rules had not: it reached past hardware to the product. It created a category for model weights and required a licence to export the weights of any closed model trained with more than 1026 computational operations, with a presumption of denial. An exception covered end users headquartered in the listed destinations, on condition of specified security measures. Open-weight models were left uncontrolled. BIS explained the line by saying that weights of smaller models were already stored around the world, so controlling them would be ineffective. That is a compute threshold used as an export trigger; part 09 covers thresholds in general.

The sequence afterwards matters. The rule took legal effect on 13 January 2025 with compliance required from 15 May 2025. On 13 May 2025 the Commerce Department announced it would be rescinded and would not be enforced. The text stayed in the regulations for another eighteen months. The July 2026 UAE rule notes that BIS was enforcing the worldwide requirement only for certain country groups, and the May 2026 guidance says the older headquarters-based requirement inside it was still live. The November 2026 rule deleted the provisions.

The AI Diffusion Rule has never been enforced.
BIS, 91 FR 74970, summary

That is BIS's description. On this map's reading, set next to the two documents above, it refers to the new 2025 requirements, not to the 2023 control that had been moved into the same paragraph. The November rule deletes the model-weights category, the country groupings and their licence exceptions, and the data-centre authorisation programme. It keeps one licence exception the 2025 rule created, for chip manufacturing supply chains. On weights, BIS says destination-based and end-use licence requirements address the concern more effectively.

January 2026: a review path for some chips to China

This rule is narrow and easy to overstate. It does not remove a licence requirement. For chips with TPP below 21,000 and total memory bandwidth below 6,500 GB/s (the rule gives the NVIDIA H200 and AMD MI325X as examples), exported from the United States to end users located in China or Macau, it changes how an application is reviewed: case by case in place of presumed denial. Re-exports from other countries, and exports to entities headquartered in D:5 destinations but located elsewhere, stay at presumption of denial.

To get the case-by-case path the exporter must certify, with supporting data, that the export will not delay orders from customers in the United States or divert foundry capacity from chips for them; that the total sent to China and Macau is no more than 50 percent of what has shipped to United States customers; that the chips are not for military or military-intelligence users and those parties will not get remote access; and that every shipment is checked by an independent testing lab in the United States to confirm it performs as declared. The application must also describe the recipient's know-your-customer procedures and physical security.

This page says nothing about how many licences have been applied for or granted, because the rule text cannot tell you that. The November 2026 rule keeps this review path in its rewritten text.

November 2026: the chip that never moves

The newest rule addresses more directly the gap the 2023 commenter described. BIS says the 2023 headquarters control already reached a restricted company that was the main remote user of a data centre; this rule adds a control keyed to volume. Its central provision is a new section, § 744.24. A licence is required to export, re-export or transfer the controlled chips when the exporter knows they are going to a data centre, anywhere in the world, that provides or will provide access to more than 12,000,000 "aggregate TPP" to or for companies headquartered in Macau or a D:5 destination, or whose ultimate parent is. The total counts across linked data centres, not just one building. Applications are reviewed case by case.

For scale, on this map's arithmetic: 12,000,000 TPP is about 2,500 chips at the 4800 TPP line where the top control class starts, or about 570 chips at the 21,000 ceiling of the January 2026 band.

Because the thing controlled is a use and not a shipment, the rule needs machinery earlier rules did not.

  • Diligence. The exporter is expected to obtain the data centre's know-your-customer checks, including whether customers are owned or controlled from a restricted destination. A data centre that will not supply them counts as a red flag that presumptively gives the exporter knowledge.
  • Later changes of use. If chips arrive lawfully and the data centre later starts serving a covered customer above the line, that change needs authorisation. A data centre already doing so must obtain a licence within 90 days.
  • One carve-out. The control does not apply when access is only for a "commercial recommender system", a tool exclusively designed and used to rank, filter or suggest products, services or content. Systems that generate novel content are expressly excluded. The exporter must report the customer and the compute provided.

The rule also adds a licence requirement for Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Brazil and Thailand, which BIS describes as destinations with an unacceptable risk of diversion. It does not apply when the consignee or end user is headquartered in the United States and the exporter files monthly reports.

A note for readers of the text: this rule writes "Super Intelligence" and "SI" where earlier rules wrote "AI", and a footnote attributes the change to an executive order. This page keeps saying AI.

What the lever can and cannot do

Part 07 set out why Sastry and co-authors think compute suits governance: it is physical, countable and made by a concentrated supply chain. Export controls are that argument applied. The same paper is frank about what kind of tool it is.

It targets users, not uses. The paper observes that traditional enforcement has to focus on who gets the chips, not on what they are used for, and so aims at whole countries and organisations. The November 2026 rule shows the cost of going further. To reach a use, BIS had to make exporters answerable for the due diligence of foreign data centres.

It acts on hardware flows, not on models. None of the rules read for this page sets a standard for how a model is built, tested or released. They concern who may have the hardware and who may use it. The one category that controlled a model directly, the 2025 weights provision, is gone, though the January 2026 certifications still restrict passing on weights trained on the licensed chips. Sastry et al. also note that a model trained on large compute can be run on much less, so denying training hardware does not reach capabilities that already exist.

Every restriction creates a route around it. The paper lists the short-term routes as chip smuggling, using chips that fall outside the controls, and accessing cloud compute. The timeline above is BIS meeting each in turn. The paper's longer-term worry is different:

Squeezing one part of the supply chain puts pressure on other parts.
Sastry et al., section 5.A

A country denied chips has a stronger reason to build a supply chain with no United States inputs. If it succeeds, the concentration that made compute governable in the first place is reduced.

It changes relative position and imposes no duty of care. On this map's reading, this is the structural point. Part 06 described race dynamics, where each competitor trims caution because of the others. An export control changes the relative position of the runners. The January 2026 rule states this aim directly: its conditions are meant to ensure the destination's computing capability does not exceed that of the United States. A rule with that aim does not ask any developer, at home or abroad, to be more careful. Part 12 covers agreements between states.

Carry this away. Export controls answer the question "who may have the hardware". They have been revised roughly yearly since 2022, and each revision follows the compute to a new place: the chip, the third country, the headquarters, the rented data centre. They are binding regulation backed by licensing, and they are silent on what gets built.

Where people disagree

The agency has disagreed with itself. The plainest disputes are on the face of the record. In January 2025 BIS concluded that a worldwide licence requirement with country allocations was necessary. In May 2025 the same department said that framework would have burdened companies and damaged relations with the countries it ranked lower. The 2025 rule treated the weights of the largest closed models as something to control in their own right; the 2026 rule says destination and end-use controls do that job better. The January 2026 rule eased review for one class of chips to China, citing United States leadership, where earlier rules presumed denial.

Critics of the controls, as Sastry et al. report them. The paper, written in early 2024, describes the reaction to the 2022 controls as mixed. It reports concern about economic cost to the United States and its allies, objections from chip makers, who stress their dependence on China for supplies, retaliatory Chinese controls on chip-making materials, and an argument that the controls came too early because keeping a rival dependent on your supply chain is itself leverage. It reports the worry that controls speed up the target's drive for independence, and the reply that China was pursuing independence before 2022 regardless. It also reports doubts about whether the controls are well targeted and enforced.

The paper's own position. Sastry et al. call cutting off compute a blunt instrument with many downsides. They note it also blocks benign research and commercial uses, and they argue for narrower tools and guardrails, which part 07 describes.

The unilateral problem. The commenter BIS summarised in 2023 made a point that still stands in the text: without a multilateral control, other states can supply the same compute. The November 2026 rule's answer is to reach data centres abroad through the chips inside them, and to say that Commerce will monitor countries that tolerate evasion. Whether that substitutes for agreement among supplier states the rule text cannot settle.

What you can say now

  • United States export controls on AI chips are agency regulations under the Export Control Reform Act, usually issued as interim final rules that bind on publication.
  • The sequence is: chips and equipment for China (October 2022), performance-density thresholds, more destinations and a headquarters test (October 2023), a worldwide framework with model-weight controls (January 2025, announced as unenforced in May 2025, deleted in November 2026), and a remote-access control on data centres (November 2026).
  • The January 2026 rule did not lift the licence requirement for China; it changed the review posture for a bounded class of chips, on conditions tied to United States supply.
  • The dedicated export category for model weights, created in January 2025, was removed in November 2026.
  • Export controls act on who holds hardware. They do not set any standard for how models are built, so on this map's reading they change who is ahead without asking anyone to be more careful.
