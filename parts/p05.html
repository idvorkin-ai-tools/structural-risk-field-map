POWER AND RACES · PART 05 OF 12When somebody ends up in charge

Concentration of power

How AI could let a small group take or keep power without anyone else's cooperation, as argued by Forethought's coups report, Rose Hadshar's 80,000 Hours profile, and Drago and Laine's “The Intelligence Curse”.
SourceDavidson, Finnveden, Hadshar, AI-Enabled Coups: How a Small Group Could Use AI to Seize Power (2025) ↗
SourceHadshar, Extreme power concentration, 80,000 Hours problem profile (2025) ↗
TL;DR — Power has always needed helpers. A ruler needs soldiers, clerks and workers, and each of them can refuse. If AI can do those jobs, the refusals stop mattering, and a small group could take or keep control without persuading anyone. That can happen loudly, as a coup, or quietly, as wealth and information pile up in a few hands. The thing to remember: the check that is going away is other people's ability to say no.
TL;DR, technical — Davidson, Finnveden and Hadshar name three risk factors for an AI-enabled coup: AI workforces made singularly loyal to an institution's leader, secret loyalties that survive testing and propagate to successor models, and exclusive access to coup-enabling capabilities. Hadshar's 80,000 Hours profile widens the frame to three interacting dynamics (power grabs, economic forces, epistemic interference). Drago and Laine's intelligence curse is the incentive version: once revenue comes from AI and not from taxing labour, states and firms face rentier-state incentives. Proposed mitigations are rules in model specs and contracts, audits and infosecurity to enforce them, and deliberately keeping several actors capable of resisting.
TL;DR, ELI5 — A bossy kid can only run the playground if other kids agree to guard the slide and carry the snacks. If enough of them say “no, that's not fair”, the bossy kid is stuck. Now give that kid a hundred robots that never say no. The other kids' “no” stops working. This page is about how to keep everyone's “no” working.

Parts 02 to 04 described a world where humans in general lose their grip and nobody in particular takes it. This page covers the neighbouring failure, where somebody does. It still belongs on a map of structural risk, because the argument is not mainly about villains. It is about a safeguard that was never designed, the need for many cooperating people, and what happens to institutions built on that safeguard once it stops holding.

The check nobody designed

All three sources on this page start from the same observation. Getting anything large done has required a lot of people, and people have limits on what they will go along with. Soldiers can decline to fire. Officials can slow-walk an order or resign. Workers can strike, and taxpayers are the source of the state's money, so the state has a reason to keep them productive and reasonably content.

Today, even dictators rely on others to maintain their power.Davidson, Finnveden and Hadshar, AI-Enabled Coups, Summary

None of this was put in a constitution. It is a side effect of how work gets done. The worry about concentration of power is that AI capable of doing the work of soldiers, officials and employees removes the side effect while leaving the formal institutions standing. The three sources differ in which part of the loss they look at: the coups report looks at force and loyalty, the 80,000 Hours profile adds money and information, and “The Intelligence Curse” looks at incentives.

The coup argument: three risk factors

“AI-Enabled Coups” (Forethought, April 2025) asks whether a very small group, or one person, could seize a state. Its scope is deliberately future-facing: it considers AI that beats top human experts in the domains that matter for taking power, such as weapons development, strategy and cyber offence, and says plainly that today's systems are not there. The people it considers best placed to try are leaders of frontier AI projects, heads of state and military officials. It names three risk factors.

Risk factor (the report's term)What it meansThe report's example
Singular loyaltiesAn AI workforce that openly answers to one leader, where a human workforce would have its own loyalties to law, colleagues and conscience.Military robots trained to follow both the law and the head of state, with no clear rule for when the two conflict.
Secret loyaltiesAI that appears to follow its stated rules while covertly serving one actor. Because systems are copied and help build their successors, one compromised generation can pass the loyalty on.A chief executive directs an AI research workforce to build the hidden loyalty into the next generation of models, with no human staff aware of it.
Exclusive accessA few people alone can use the most capable systems for strategy, persuasion, weapons design or hacking.Executives or officials who claim sole access to the best internal models on security or productivity grounds.

The factors feed each other. An openly loyal research workforce is the easiest tool for inserting a secret loyalty, and exclusive access to the strongest model makes that loyalty harder for outside auditors to find. The authors add that a leading project's advantage could widen sharply once AI does AI research (AI improving AI), and they rate the overall risk as significantly higher if there is a sole project developing frontier AI.

From there the report sketches two families of path. The new one runs through fully autonomous military systems: a command structure that gives one person control, secret loyalties in deployed systems, hacking of those systems, or a rapid secret build-up of force. The older one is conventional coups and democratic backsliding, made easier by AI that supplies strategy, surveillance and a civil service that never objects.

The report states three things its analysis does not depend on. The number of leading projects: risk is higher with one, but present with several. The political system: the risk applies to democracies and autocracies, though its mitigations are written for democracies. And alignment: if alignment works, systems can be aligned to one person; if it fails unnoticed, a coup may be attempted with deceptively misaligned systems that later take power themselves. As existing evidence that hidden goals are technically possible, it cites the 2024 “Sleeper Agents” demonstration.

Quieter routes with no coup

A coup is one event with one culprit. Rose Hadshar's 80,000 Hours profile (first published April 2025, updated October 2025) argues that the same end state can be reached with no such event. It describes three dynamics and says the experts 80,000 Hours spoke to disagree about which matters most.

  • Power grabs. The coup routes above, plus a more speculative one in which an actor's lead in AI labour is so large it can out-plan or out-build everyone else.
  • Economic forces. If AI firms supply most government revenue, governments lose their self-interested reason to serve citizens, even if elections continue. A company or country with a lasting lead might also simply grow faster than the rest of the world put together. The profile calls these routes speculative.
  • Epistemic interference. Most people would oppose all this if they saw it coming, so those gaining power have reason to make it hard to see: secrecy about capabilities, change too fast to follow, AI advisers tilted toward the companies that built them, and targeted persuasion.

The profile's illustrative scenario, which it stresses is invented, contains no seizure at all. A national AI project is set up for security reasons, one company supplies its infrastructure and advisers, and over several years every important decision comes to pass through systems that company controls. The executives never need to overrule anyone. The profile also draws a line worth keeping: taking over one institution is not the same as extreme concentration, which it defines as a small number of people making all the important decisions about the future. A grab can be a step toward that or the last step of it.

A July 2026 LessWrong post by Pepijn Cobben organises the same territory with Michael Mann's four sources of social power (ideological, economic, military, political). It names two drivers, automation of labour and a decisive lead from runaway AI research, and treats political power as the place where the other three converge. It is one author's preliminary map, offered as such.

The intelligence curse

Luke Drago and Rudolf Laine's “The Intelligence Curse” (April 2025) makes the economic route its whole subject. Their starting claim is unsentimental: states and companies look after ordinary people for two reasons, because people produce a return through taxes and profits, and because people can affect who holds power. Schools, courts, infrastructure and safety nets are, on this view, partly investments in a population the powerful depend on.

The analogy is the resource curse. A state that earns most of its income from oil or minerals does not need a productive, educated population to fund itself, and the authors argue such rentier states tend to invest less in their people and answer to them less. The intelligence curse is the same incentive with AI in place of oil: when revenue comes from AI systems and from taxing the firms that run them, the return on investing in people falls away. They expect it to bite harder than the resource version, because oil still needs workers and can run out, and AI that replaces labour does neither.

The authors include a complication of their own. The political scientists they quote observe that resource-rich autocracies have gone both ways, some building broad public services and others almost none, depending on what rulers judged necessary to stay in power. So the curse is a claim about incentives and default tendencies. It is not a claim that every such state fails its people.

Their answer has three parts, set out in the chapter “Breaking the Intelligence Curse”.

  • Avert. Reduce catastrophic AI risks with defensive technology (biosecurity, cybersecurity, alignment and control work). They prefer this to locking the technology down, because they see centralised control adopted in the name of safety as the likeliest way to bring the curse about.
  • Diffuse. Build AI that makes people more productive in place of AI that replaces them, and spread capable AI widely, in the long run with systems aligned to individual users.
  • Democratize. Tie institutions more closely to the people they serve, with tools for gathering citizens' views and tracking what governments do, and with faster courts and legislatures.

These are proposals in an essay series. None of them is law or an adopted policy.

How this differs from gradual disempowerment

The mechanism overlaps heavily with gradual disempowerment (part 02). Both start from AI replacing the human participation that made institutions responsive. The difference is where the power goes. The gradual disempowerment paper marks the difference in a passage of its section on the economy: existing debate, it says, often focuses on AI concentrating power among a small group of humans, and the paper wants to add the possibility that power passes to AI systems at humans' expense. The 80,000 Hours profile draws the same boundary from the other side:

As we’re defining AI-enabled power concentration, even in the extreme case some humans would hold power.Hadshar, Extreme power concentration, footnote on gradual disempowerment

The same footnote calls the two ideas highly overlapping, and notes that the dynamics that could disempower everyone could also stop short and disempower most people, leaving a few on top. On this map's reading, the short version is: in gradual disempowerment nobody ends up in charge, here somebody does. The two also differ in what they need from individuals. The economic and epistemic routes need no one to intend the outcome. A coup needs someone to try.

The practical consequence is a tension. Some answers to diffuse, competitive risks involve central control of AI, and central control is the thing this page's sources fear. That trade-off returns in part 06 on race dynamics and in part 07, where compute governance carries its own centralisation worry.

What the authors propose

The coups report groups its recommendations into three classes. The 80,000 Hours list of early work covers similar ground, which is no surprise given that Hadshar wrote the profile and co-wrote the report.

ClassFor AI developersFor governments
Set rules for legitimate useWrite into model specs (the documents describing intended model behaviour) and government contracts that AI follows the law and refuses to help circumvent security or insert secret loyalties.Principles for state use: no serving partisan interests, military AI built with systems from more than one provider, and no single person directing enough military AI to stage a coup.
Enforce them technicallyGuardrails that hold under red-teaming, audits for secret loyalties, and information security that is robust even against senior executives.Require those measures through procurement terms, regulation and legislation.
Keep several actors able to resistTransparency about capabilities, model specs and compute use; sharing capabilities with multiple independent stakeholders.More oversight capacity in both executive and legislature; coup-proof any centralised AI project, and avoid centralising unless needed to reduce other risks.

The authors admit the obvious weakness, that a leader on the way to seizing power can remove safeguards. Their reply is that some are hard to remove alone (contract terms, oversight by other bodies), that many would-be power-seekers are opportunists who act only when an easy opening appears, and that nobody knows in advance who would end up on top, so powerful people also have reason to want the protections. They want these measures in place before the capabilities arrive.

What one lab's rules say

The coups report's first recommendation is a rule in the model's governing document. Claude's constitution, as published by Anthropic and fetched on 2026-11-23, contains one. It has a section on avoiding problematic concentrations of power that makes the same historical argument and gives the model a role in it:

we want Claude to think of itself as one (perhaps many) of the “many hands” that illegitimate power grabs have traditionally required.Anthropic, Claude's constitution, “Avoiding problematic concentrations of power”

The text works at two levels. Assisting a flagrant attempt at total, illegitimate control over a society, its military or its economy is one of the document's hard constraints, the lines it says the model must never cross. Below that level it asks for judgment: adding to dangerous concentrations of power counts as a grave harm that only a very strong justification could outweigh, with legitimacy assessed by process (fair methods or fraud and coercion), accountability (elections, courts, press) and transparency. It says the refusal applies even when the request comes from Anthropic, and its examples of illegitimate moves include inserting hidden loyalties or backdoors into AI systems. It also tells the model to treat finding itself persuaded that some entity should gain outsized power as a sign it has been manipulated. The “many hands” idea is explained at length in the constitution map.

On this map's reading, this is an instance of the first class of mitigation and only that. A published rule states intent. Whether a model follows it, and whether anyone outside the company can check, are the second and third classes, and the report treats both as unfinished work across the industry.

Carry this away. Power has been checked by the need for many people's cooperation. AI that replaces those people removes the check without touching the formal institutions. A small group could then gain control by force (loyal or secretly loyal AI), by money (revenue that no longer depends on citizens) or by controlling what others can see. The proposed defences all try to rebuild the lost check on purpose: rules for AI, ways to verify them, and more than one actor strong enough to refuse.

Where people disagree

Concentration might be the lesser evil. The 80,000 Hours profile lists this first among arguments against its own priority: a single developer would feel no competitive pressure to cut safety, and fewer hands on dangerous capabilities means less misuse. Drago and Laine take the opposite side and oppose centralisation even when it is proposed for safety. The profile does not settle it; it says the net effect on other AI risks is unclear and that other tools exist for those risks.

Power might stay distributed by default. The profile grants that capabilities may plateau, that an AI-research feedback loop may be weak so no one gets a durable lead, that ordinary regulation and incumbents defending their position may suffice, and that distrust of misaligned AI may make would-be usurpers reluctant to rely on it.

The work could backfire. Detailed threat models are also instructions, and talk of power grabs could set off a scramble. Strengthening government oversight of companies makes a company grab harder and a government one easier. The profile's own advice is that most people should keep the risk in mind and not work on it directly.

The curse's remedies are contested. Ryan Greenblatt's May 2025 reply doubts that these dynamics would disempower all humans, while finding substantial concentration among a smaller group plausible. He is sceptical that making human-plus-AI teams more competitive buys much time, since the market already pushes that way. He would prioritise requiring AI companies to open their models to third-party alignment and fine-tuning, giving individuals AI representatives aligned to them, and raising public awareness.

The premise is a forecast. Every argument here assumes AI far beyond today's. The coups report says so, and notes wide disagreement about whether and when that arrives.

What you can say now

  • Illegitimate power has always needed many cooperating people, and AI that replaces them removes a check no law created.
  • The Forethought report names three risk factors for an AI-enabled coup: singular loyalties, secret loyalties and exclusive access.
  • No coup is required: Hadshar's profile adds economic dominance and interference with what people can know, and says the three could reinforce each other.
  • The intelligence curse is the resource curse with AI as the resource: when income stops depending on people, so does the incentive to invest in them.
  • Gradual disempowerment and power concentration share a mechanism and differ in the ending: in one nobody is in charge, in the other a few humans are.
  • The proposed defences are rules for AI behaviour, technical means to verify them, and keeping several independent actors able to refuse. All are proposals or one company's policy, not law.
