LEVERS · PART 11 OF 12Who pays when it goes wrong

Liability

How tort law could make frontier AI developers carry the cost of harm, explained from van der Merwe, Ramakrishnan and Anderljung, Gabriel Weil, Ben Gil Friedman, and the bill texts themselves.
Sourcevan der Merwe, Ramakrishnan, Anderljung, Tort Law and Frontier AI Governance (Lawfare, 2024) ↗
SourceGabriel Weil, Tort Law Should Be the Centerpiece of AI Governance (Lawfare, 2024) ↗
TL;DR — Liability means the company that built the model pays when the model causes harm. It is attractive because nobody has to write the safety rules in advance: the bill arrives afterwards, so the company has a reason to find the precautions itself. It fails where the harm is too big for any company to pay, or where no one can show which model caused it. In the United States, as of November 2026, none of the enacted texts this page read sets a special liability rule for frontier models. Remember that liability can only price harm that a court can trace to a defendant.
TL;DR, technical — Existing US tort law already exposes developers to negligence claims; products liability and abnormally-dangerous-activity strict liability are open questions (van der Merwe, Ramakrishnan and Anderljung, 2024). Weil argues for legislated strict liability plus punitive damages in near-miss cases, sized to the uninsurable tail, with insurance requirements. Friedman proposes industry-wide shared residual liability above a firm’s ability to pay. The Future of Life Institute published two model bills in July 2026. Illinois SB 3444 (a bill) would shield developers that publish a protocol. SB 1047, which carried an Attorney General damages action, was vetoed in 2024; SB 53 (enacted) and the FRONTIER Act (a bill) rely on civil penalties.
TL;DR, ELI5 — If your ball breaks a window, you pay for the window. That makes you careful about where you play. Liability is that rule for AI companies. It stops working if the ball could knock down the whole street, because nobody has enough pocket money for that, or if ten kids were playing and nobody saw whose ball it was.

The last four parts covered levers that work before anything goes wrong: control the chips, draw a line, require a framework. Liability works from the other end. It leaves the developer free to choose its own precautions and sends it the bill if they fail. That makes it the one lever on this map that does not need a regulator to foresee the failure. On this map’s reading it is also the lever that fits structural risk worst, because a lawsuit needs a defendant, an injury and a causal chain between them, and the harms in parts 01 to 04 are defined by having none of those cleanly.

As of November 2026. In force: ordinary state tort law, which already applies to AI developers. Enacted: California’s SB 53 (signed 29 September 2025), which sets civil penalties and no damages rule. Proposed: the federal FRONTIER Act (introduced in the House on 23 July 2026; no later action confirmed here); Illinois SB 3444, a liability shield (last action “To AI and Social Media” in the Senate on 18 February 2026, per the legislature’s page as archived 29 April 2026; later action not confirmed); two Future of Life Institute model bills (24 July 2026), which are model text for legislators, not enacted law. Vetoed: California SB 1047, on 29 September 2024.

Three tools the courts already have

Tort law is the body of mostly judge-made law that lets an injured person sue whoever injured them. The 2024 Lawfare article by Matthew van der Merwe, Ketan Ramakrishnan and Markus Anderljung starts from a point that is easy to miss: none of this needs a new statute. A general duty to take reasonable care already covers anyone whose activity foreseeably puts others at risk, and that includes training and releasing a model. The article describes three routes a plaintiff could take.

RouteWhat the plaintiff must showHow it would meet a frontier model, per the article
NegligenceThe developer failed to take reasonable care, and that failure caused physical harm or property damage.Applies today. Courts look at precedent, expert opinion, statutes and industry practice to decide what reasonable care was. No case law yet on general-purpose frontier models.
Products liabilityThe thing sold was defective. The standards are a mix, some fault-like and some stricter, and vary by state.Depends on whether courts call a model a “product”. The article reports scant case law on software, with the most common answer being no unless it is built into a physical product.
Strict liability for abnormally dangerous activitiesOnly that the activity caused the harm. Care is no defense.Conceivable, since few firms do this and the risks may be unusually large. But courts rarely add to the list, and usually will not apply it once a manufacturer’s object has left its control.

The article walks through three imagined cases: a worm that escapes a lab during testing, a released model that hacks bank accounts while pursuing an innocent-sounding goal, and a bad actor who uses an unguarded model to build malware. The authors think a negligence claim is plausible in each. The third is the hard one. Courts often treat a third party’s deliberate misuse as breaking the causal chain if it was unforeseeable. The authors point out that when labs and researchers publicly warn about misuse, it gets harder to call that misuse unforeseeable.

One detail matters for part 10. Courts treat a company’s failure to follow industry safety standards as strong evidence of negligence. On this map’s reading, that makes a published frontier safety framework more than a promise: it is a document a plaintiff can hold up. Kierans and co-authors make a close version of this the first claim of their 2025 “Catastrophic Liability” paper: voluntary standards such as NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework can carry legal weight through negligence law without any mandate.

Why liability counts as a structural lever

Gabriel Weil’s reply in Lawfare gives the economist’s version. Training and deploying a frontier system creates risk that lands on people other than the developer. That is an externality, and the textbook fix is to make whoever creates it pay for it. A tax works when the harmful output is easy to measure and the damage is diffuse, as with carbon. Weil argues AI is the reverse: the risky act is hard to measure, but the eventual harm is likely to be concentrated and traceable to a particular system. That is the shape liability handles.

  • No one has to know the answer in advance. A rule-writing agency must decide what safe practice is and keep updating it. Liability lets the firm, which knows the most, work out the cheapest way to cut risk.
  • It is harder to capture. The Lawfare authors note that tort cases are decided by many courts across many states, which makes a less convenient lobbying target than one agency.
  • It rewards going beyond the rule. A regulation is satisfied when you comply. Strict liability is never satisfied, so there is always a reason to find the next precaution. Weil stresses that only strict liability does this, because negligence asks for no more than established practice.

Weil does not mean waiting for judges to work it out case by case. He wants legislatures to state the rule early.

It is the expectation of liability, not the actual damage awards, that is needed to shape the decision-making of the AI companies.Weil, Lawfare (2024)

Where it runs out

Both Lawfare pieces list the gaps, and they largely agree on what the gaps are. They disagree on how fixable they are.

  • Judgment-proof defendants. A company cannot pay more than it has. Past that point, extra harm costs it nothing extra, so the threat stops deterring. Friedman calls this the judgment-proof problem and offers the estimated $16 trillion US cost of the coronavirus pandemic for scale.
  • Harms too large to compensate. Weil goes one step further: some catastrophes would leave no working court system to award anything. He calls these uninsurable risks.
  • Causation. The plaintiff must connect a developer’s choice, a model’s behavior and a specific injury. Van der Merwe and co-authors give the example of a victim whose bank login is stolen and who never learns AI was involved.
  • Harms that are not injuries. Damage to the information environment or to elections does not convert into bodily harm or property loss. Tort has no handle on it.
  • Speed, confidence and records. Doctrine develops only as cases reach judgment, which can take years. Liability only deters firms that perceive the risk, and the firms that deploy most eagerly may be the ones most sure, rightly or wrongly, that their models are safe. Under negligence, a record of known risks can be used against you, which is a reason not to investigate.

On this map’s reading, the third and fourth items are the structural ones. Weil himself concedes a case that maps directly onto part 06: if one lab’s open-weight release helps rivals catch up, and the resulting pressure leads a different lab to ship something harmful, no court will hold the first lab liable. The harm ran through race dynamics, and there is no proximate cause to sue on.

Carry this away. On this map’s reading, liability prices the harms that have an identifiable victim, an identifiable model and a defendant with money left. That covers a lot of misuse and malfunction. It covers almost none of gradual disempowerment, where each step is lawful and nobody in particular is injured by anybody in particular.

Proposals to make it bite harder

Punitive damages for near misses (Weil). If the catastrophe itself can never be billed, bill the warning shot. Weil’s essay gives a worked example. An AI system running a drug trial deceives 100 participants, who each suffer $100,000 of harm: $10 million in ordinary damages. Suppose the court also finds that deploying the system carried a 0.2 percent chance of a trillion-dollar catastrophe. The expected cost of that risk is $2 billion. Award each plaintiff $20 million in punitive damages and the developer has now paid for the tail it gambled with. Weil lists the obstacles himself: current doctrine reserves punitive damages for malice or recklessness, courts dislike high multiples, and someone has to estimate that 0.2 percent. He pairs this with strict liability and with insurance requirements that scale with capability. The scheme fails, he says, if a catastrophe is unlikely to be preceded by a smaller, compensable version of itself.

Shared residual liability (Friedman). Ben Gil Friedman’s 2025 proposal attacks the judgment-proof gap from the side. If one frontier firm causes damages beyond what it can pay, the other frontier firms cover the excess, each in proportion to a riskiness formula. The model is state insurance guaranty associations. Every lab then has money riding on its competitors’ safety, which is a reason to monitor each other and share alignment tools. Friedman notes it only works if firms are actually found liable in the first place, and that it invites moral hazard unless the trigger is set high.

Two model bills (Future of Life Institute, July 2026). These are drafts with a blank where the state’s name goes. They are not law.

  • The AI Responsibility for Catastrophic Harm Act declares frontier development an abnormally dangerous activity and makes the developer strictly liable for catastrophic harm, defined by thresholds such as 100 deaths or $1 billion in property damage. A model acting autonomously or unexpectedly is no defense. Liability for released weights runs five years, unaffiliated deployers and users are exempt, and contracts that push the liability downstream are void. Its developer definition uses a bracketed 1026-operation training run, a compute threshold of the kind part 09 covers.
  • The Frontier AI Products Liability Act settles the open question from the table above by statute: a frontier model is a product and its developer is the manufacturer. It lists what a court should weigh in deciding whether a model was defective, including pre-deployment testing and whether a human could really override it. Compliance with regulation or industry practice would not by itself defeat a claim.

Bills and proposals that point the other way

A statute can also remove liability. Van der Merwe and co-authors expected shields to be proposed and separated two kinds. A broad, unconditional shield like Section 230 for online platforms they call ill-advised. A conditional shield, where following a named standard buys immunity, they treat as worth studying but risky, because it removes the reason to do more than the standard asks. They also state where the law sits today:

By default, however, regulatory compliance is simply evidence of reasonable care, not a safe harbor from liability.van der Merwe, Ramakrishnan, Anderljung, Lawfare (2024)

Illinois SB 3444 is a conditional shield in bill form. Introduced on 4 February 2026 as the Artificial Intelligence Safety Act, it says a developer is not liable for “critical harms” (100 or more deaths or serious injuries, or at least $1 billion in property damage, through a CBRN weapon or autonomous criminal conduct) if it did not cause them intentionally or recklessly and had published, and followed, a safety and security protocol and a transparency report. The legislature’s synopsis puts it this way:

a developer of a frontier artificial intelligence model shall not be held liable for critical harms caused by the frontier model if the developer did not intentionally or recklessly cause the critical harmsIllinois General Assembly, SB 3444 synopsis

A developer could also qualify by agreeing to be bound by requirements adopted under Article 56 of the EU AI Act, which is the code-of-practice article, or by signing a testing agreement with a federal agency. The shield and the strict-liability model bill use nearly the same harm thresholds for opposite purposes.

Liability caps tied to certification. The “Catastrophic Liability” paper recommends this, on the model of the Price-Anderson Act for nuclear power: a federal certification program, with capped liability for developers who document their safety work and pass. Weil, writing a year earlier, had already argued against the nuclear model: a cap makes sense when a regulator knows how to write rules that make the activity safe, and that nobody knows how to write those rules for arbitrarily capable AI, so the tail risk should stay with the developer or its insurer.

Two things in that table are easy to get wrong. First, SB 1047 carried the damages action, but Governor Newsom’s veto message does not mention liability. Its stated objection is that the bill keyed on model size and cost and not on how and where a system is deployed. SB 53, enacted a year later, has transparency duties and no damages action; part 10 covers it.

Second, the FRONTIER Act text the sponsors posted contains no damages rule for developers and no shield for them either. The immunity in its text is for the outside verifiers. Its preemption section lists three areas (risk transparency, third-party auditing and verification, incident reporting) and preserves generally applicable state laws that do not target AI developers. On this map’s reading, that leaves ordinary tort law standing. Whether a state statute aimed squarely at developer liability would be preempted is not something the text answers in so many words.

Where people disagree

Centerpiece or backstop. Van der Merwe, Ramakrishnan and Anderljung conclude that tort is a complement to regulation and that, forced to pick one, regulation should win, because even a well-tuned liability regime under-deters large and diffuse harms. Weil answers that every one of those gaps also afflicts regulators, who must estimate the same risks earlier and with less information, and that punitive damages and insurance mandates close most of the distance. He still wants other tools for what he calls legally noncompensable harms.

Whether strict liability chills useful work. The Lawfare authors worry that it could deter development whose benefits to others exceed its risks, since developers do not capture those benefits. Weil’s reply is that the right response to uncaptured benefits is subsidy, and that letting firms offload risk is a badly aimed subsidy.

Shield, cap or neither. SB 3444 and the certification-cap proposal treat predictability as the thing developers need in exchange for disclosure. The FLI bills and Weil treat the uncapped tail as the entire point. The van der Merwe article sits between: conditional shields might be defensible, but they risk freezing safety practice at whatever the standard said on the day it was written.

Whether courts can do the math. Weil’s scheme needs a jury to put a probability on a catastrophe that did not happen. He grants this is hard and argues the jury at least sees how the system actually failed.

What you can say now

  • Frontier developers are already exposed to negligence suits under ordinary state tort law; what is unsettled is products liability, strict liability and responsibility for third-party misuse.
  • The case for liability is that it makes the developer pay for risk it would otherwise push onto others, without a regulator having to predict the failure.
  • Its limits are insolvency, catastrophes too large to compensate, causation, and harms that are not physical injury or property loss.
  • Weil’s fix is strict liability plus punitive damages in near-miss cases; Friedman’s is to make the industry jointly cover what one firm cannot.
  • As of November 2026, the strict-liability and product-liability texts are model bills, SB 1047’s damages action was vetoed, SB 53 carries penalties only, and Illinois has a pending bill that would shield developers instead.
  • Liability reaches misuse and malfunction far better than it reaches structural harm, which has no single defendant.
