POWER AND RACES · PART 06 OF 12Why rivals each cut caution

Race dynamics

How competition lowers everyone's safety at once: Armstrong, Bostrom and Shulman's race model, what two labs' own policies say about competitors, and the argument between Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang's deterrence proposal and Katzke and Futerman's case against a national race.
SourceArmstrong, Bostrom & Shulman, Racing to the Precipice: a Model of Artificial Intelligence Development (FHI technical report 2013; AI & Society 2016) ↗
SourceHendrycks, Schmidt & Wang, Superintelligence Strategy: Expert Version (2025) ↗
TL;DR — When being first matters a lot, each competitor gives up some caution to avoid losing, and all of them end up less careful than any of them wanted. A small 2013 model shows the pattern and what makes it worse: more rivals, more hostility between them, and a contest where taking risks buys more than skill does. Two AI labs now write the same logic into their safety policies, tying what they promise to what competitors do. Between states, one camp proposes a stand-off held in place by the threat of sabotage and another argues that a national race defeats its own purpose. The thing to remember: you cannot fix a race by asking one runner to be careful.
TL;DR, technical — Armstrong, Bostrom and Shulman model n teams with capability drawn uniformly on [0, μ] choosing safety s in [0, 1]; the highest c − s wins and avoids disaster with probability s. In the Nash equilibrium, risk falls as μ rises and as enmity e falls, the no-information case is always safest, and extra teams strictly raise risk in the no-information and public-information cases. Anthropic's RSP v3.4 (Appendix A) and OpenAI's Preparedness Framework v2 (§4.3) both make safeguards explicitly conditional on competitors. At state level, "Superintelligence Strategy" describes Mutual Assured AI Malfunction as a default deterrence regime; "The Manhattan Trap" argues the race is a trust dilemma, not a prisoner's dilemma. RAND and Wildeford and Delaney dispute MAIM's feasibility and stability.
TL;DR, ELI5 — Imagine kids racing across a frozen pond for one big prize. Walking slowly is safe, but the slow kid loses, so everybody runs, and running is how the ice breaks. No kid wants the ice to break. Telling one kid to slow down does not help, because the others keep running. You have to change the race itself: fewer racers, a prize they can share, or a rule they can all see being kept.

Parts 01 to 04 described harm that nobody chose. Part 05 described what happens if somebody ends up in charge. This page is about the pressure that sits between the two: a contest in which every participant would prefer more caution, and each still chooses less. It is the cleanest case of structural risk, because you can swap out any single actor for a more careful one and the outcome barely moves.

Structure: why a careful actor cannot afford care

Remco Zwetsloot and Allan Dafoe's 2019 Lawfare essay gives the frame. The usual way to think about AI harm asks who misused the system or which engineer made a mistake. Both questions assume there is one agent whose behaviour, if changed, would have prevented the harm. The structural view starts from the opposite assumption: in many situations the risk stays about the same after you change any one agent.

As with an avalanche, it may be more useful to ask what caused the slope to become so steep, rather than what specific event set it off. Zwetsloot & Dafoe, 2019

Their example is the fatal crash of a self-driving car in 2018. The first explanation offered was a brittle vision system. As the essay tells it, later investigation found the car had detected the victim in time, and the emergency braking had been switched off on purpose because engineers feared that a twitchy brake would make the vehicle look worse than competitors'. More and better engineers might have helped, the authors allow, but would not have been enough. The pressure came from the market and from inside the company.

That is race dynamics in one incident. Safety competes with looking good against a rival, and the rival is under the same pressure. The essay's conclusion is that when the situation itself pushes people toward risk, the effective response is to change the situation for everyone at once, which means collective rules. It adds that this is harder between countries than inside one, because inside a country legislatures, agencies and courts can set rules that bind all firms, and between countries no such authority exists.

The model: Racing to the Precipice

Stuart Armstrong, Nick Bostrom and Carl Shulman turned this intuition into a small game. This page read the 2013 technical report; the paper was later published in the journal AI & Society (volume 31, issue 2, 2016, by its DOI record).

Several teams race to build the first powerful AI. Each has some skill, and each chooses how careful to be. Being less careful makes a team faster. Whoever is fastest builds the AI, and the chance that it ends in disaster is exactly as large as the caution the winner gave up. Every team wants to win, and every team would rather see nobody have a disaster. The paper then asks where the teams settle when each is responding to the others.

There are n teams. Each has capability c, drawn independently and uniformly from [0, μ], and picks a safety level s between 0 and 1. The team with the highest c − s wins. The winner's project succeeds with probability s and causes a disaster with probability 1 − s. A team gets utility 1 for its own success, 0 for a disaster and 1 − e if another team succeeds, where e is enmity. In the no-information case the symmetric equilibrium is s = μ / (e·n) when that is below 1: shading safety by a little raises the chance of winning by that amount over μ, worth s·e, and costs that amount times the 1/n chance of having won anyway. With public information the leader's safety is the gap to the runner-up divided by e, capped at 1.

Picture the frozen pond again. Each kid picks how fast to run. Faster means more likely to win and more likely to crack the ice. The paper works out how fast everyone ends up running when each kid is watching the others.

Three numbers do the work. The number of teams. Enmity, which is how much a team minds a rival winning safely: at zero it does not care who succeeds, at one a rival's success is as bad as a disaster. And the relative weight of skill against risk-taking: if skill dominates, skimping on safety buys little; if it does not, skimping can decide the race.

The teams are assumed to end at a Nash equilibrium, the point where nobody gains by changing their own choice alone. The paper suggests how they get there: each trims safety a little in response to the others, then a little more.

What the model says

ChangeEffect on disaster risk in the model
Skill matters more than risk-takingLower, in every version. When risk-taking is nearly all that matters, the only equilibrium is no precautions at all.
Less enmity between teamsLower, always.
More teamsHigher when teams know nothing about capabilities, and higher when capabilities are public. When each team knows only its own, more teams is worse at low capability and better at high capability.
More information about capabilitiesKnowing nothing is always the safest case. Between private and public knowledge, the ranking depends on enmity and on how much skill matters.

The information result is the surprising one. The paper's formulas show part of the mechanism. If everyone knows everyone's capability, the leader knows exactly how small its margin is, and a close runner-up forces it to cut. A leader with a wide margin can afford to be safe; a neck-and-neck race is the dangerous one. The authors call this an information hazard.

The paper is careful about what follows. It says there is little anyone can do about how much skill matters, except to steer research toward approaches where risk-taking pays less. Enmity can be worked on by building trust, sharing discoveries or merging projects. Fewer teams helps in most cases, so encouraging mergers is the lever it names.

The limits are stated in the paper too. Every team feels the same enmity toward every other, safety levels are known with certainty, and the game jumps straight to equilibrium. On this map's reading, three further assumptions carry most of the weight: safety is one dial that trades directly against speed, the first finisher takes everything, and the outcome of the winner's project is all that counts. Where those hold only loosely, the model is a picture of a pressure and not a forecast.

Labs: what their own documents say about competitors

You do not have to infer that labs see this pressure. Two of them say so in the text of their voluntary frontier safety frameworks.

Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, version 3.4 (effective 8 July 2026, the version its page links as current in November 2026), describes its own redesign as driven by a collective action problem. The previous version committed to bringing its models' risk to acceptable levels regardless of what others did. The current one separates what the company plans to do whatever others do from a more demanding set of "recommendations for industry-wide safety", and says it cannot commit to following the latter unilaterally. Its stated reason:

the developers with the weakest protections would set the pace, and responsible developers would lose their ability to do safety research and advance the public benefit. Anthropic RSP v3.4, Introduction

Its Appendix A, "Commitments Related to Competitors", then lists three cases. If the company is clearly in the lead, it will require a strong argument that catastrophic risk is contained and delay as needed, until it no longer believes its lead is significant. If it has strong evidence that all competitors at the frontier can make such arguments, it will meet or exceed their overall posture, again delaying as needed. If one competitor adopts a clearly better mitigation at comparable cost, it will make a significant effort to match it, without promising to delay. The appendix calls these commitments high-level and limited, and says they do not rule out pausing in other cases.

OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, version 2 (dated 15 April 2025) has a section titled "Marginal risk". If another frontier developer releases a system with High or Critical capability without comparable safeguards, and OpenAI can rigorously confirm it, the company says it could adjust its own required safeguards. The conditions are that doing so does not meaningfully raise overall risk of severe harm, that it says so publicly, and that it stays more protective than the other developer, a condition the text ties to avoiding a race to the bottom.

METR's compilation of these policies (December 2025 version) adds one more instance: it quotes Google DeepMind's framework recommending its Security Level 4 for one capability threshold while saying this has to be taken on by the frontier AI field as a whole.

On this map's reading, these clauses are the race model written as policy: each lab's safety level is a stated function of its lead and of its rivals' behaviour. Notice also what the lead-dependent clause does. It promises more caution with a wide margin and less with a narrow one, which is the public-information result from the 2013 paper. Per-lab detail on thresholds and reports is in the preparedness-reports comparison.

States: a harder version of the same game

Between states the contest changes in two ways. The prize is framed as military, and there is no shared regulator. Zwetsloot and Dafoe quote the policymaker Bob Work saying the only way his side goes down the path of autonomous weapons is if adversaries do and it finds itself at an operational disadvantage. That is a decision-maker describing his own caution as conditional on a rival's.

Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang's "Superintelligence Strategy" (2025) starts from that world. It rejects three existing answers: no restrictions at all; a voluntary moratorium, which it calls toothless without verification; and a single national project to win a monopoly, which it expects rivals to see coming and move against. Its replacement is Mutual Assured AI Malfunction, or MAIM.

The argument runs like this. A rival racing for a decisive lead threatens you whether it succeeds or loses control, so you will not wait. Large AI projects are easy to interfere with: the paper lists a ladder from espionage, through covert tampering with training and theft of model weights, to open cyberattacks, strikes on datacentres and, at the top, wider hostilities. Since every would-be monopolist must expect that, none makes the attempt. The authors present this as close to the situation that already exists and as something to maintain deliberately: make the escalation steps clear, build datacentres away from cities, add transparency and inspection so ordinary AI services are not mistaken for a dash to dominance. The paper's own FAQ says it is not advocating attacks on other countries' facilities. It pairs deterrence with non-proliferation, including chip controls (part 08), and with domestic competitiveness. The prize it expects states to chase is what it calls an intelligence recursion, a recursive self-improvement loop of AI doing AI research, which it says tempts states to accept bigger risks of losing control.

The Manhattan Trap: the race undercuts its own goal

Corin Katzke and Gideon Futerman's "The Manhattan Trap" (December 2024) attacks the case for a national race from the inside. It grants the two things the case needs, that superintelligence would give a decisive military advantage and that states are rational and well informed, and then asks what else those assumptions imply.

The paper finds three barriers. First, rivals who believe the same thing must treat your project as a threat to their survival, so they have reason to disrupt it or steal from it before it finishes. Second, a system strong enough to overpower great-power militaries is a mortal danger to its own builder if control fails, and the fast take-off needed for a decisive advantage is the setting where control is hardest. Third, even a controlled system hands a few people a decisive edge over their own country, which is the concentration of power that part 05 covers, and a project run fast and in secret is badly placed to prevent it. The authors do not estimate how likely loss of control is; the claim is conditional.

From there the paper changes the game being played. In a prisoner's dilemma, racing is your best move whatever the other side does. The authors argue this contest is a trust dilemma instead: both sides prefer mutual restraint to mutual racing, and each races only if it expects the other to. Such a game has two stable outcomes, and which one you land in depends on confidence. Verification supplies confidence without changing anyone's payoffs, and the paper argues it need not be perfect, since the gap between the two outcomes is so large. How such agreements and inspections might work is part 12's subject.

On this map's reading, the two papers agree on more than their titles suggest. Both expect a Manhattan-style project to provoke sabotage or worse. They differ on what holds the restraint in place: the threat of sabotage in one, mutual visibility in the other.

Carry this away. A race is a property of the contest, not of the contestants. The variables that matter are how many are running, how much each minds the other winning, how much speed can be bought with risk, and what each can see of the others. Every proposal on this page is an attempt to move one of those four.

Where people disagree

Whether MAIM would work. Three RAND political scientists, Iskander Rehman, Karl Mueller and Michael Mazarr, argue that it is neither feasible nor stabilising. AI development may not sit in a few targets that can be found and disabled. Nobody can tell when a rival has crossed the line that would justify acting. And the threat is only to destroy a programme that the rival would also lose by stopping, so it deters little. They also say the analogy runs backwards: nuclear deterrence held because neither side could prevent the other's capability, whereas MAIM rests on being able to. A standing ability to disable a rival creates a reason to strike first.

The result could be a hair-trigger balance of AI terror. Rehman, Mueller & Mazarr, RAND, 2025

Peter Wildeford and Oscar Delaney make a similar case: AI progress is gradual, so there is no obvious red line; attacks may not work and may themselves be deterred by the risk of escalation; and the paper slides between describing how states will behave and recommending how they should.

Hendrycks and Adam Khoja replied in September 2025. Their main points: most of these dangers come from racing toward superintelligence itself, with or without MAIM; red lines can be imprecise and still deter, and fully autonomous AI research is a usable one; and the comparison with nuclear deterrence was a teaching aid, since MAIM is about preventing a capability and not retaliating for its use. They present deterrence as a first step toward verified agreements.

Whether the prize is winner-take-all. The race model, the case for racing and MAIM all assume a large first-mover advantage. Wildeford and Delaney doubt the gap would be that large, noting that a rival could steal a model or protect its nuclear deterrent. "The Manhattan Trap" itself points out that if advanced AI spreads gradually through economies, the leader may never pull far enough ahead. If so, the incentive to cut safety is weaker than the model implies. In the model's own terms, that is a contest where skimping buys less.

Whether states are rational enough. The trust-dilemma argument needs both sides to understand the dangers and to believe the other does. Its authors name political will, not technology, as the main barrier. On this map's reading that is also its soft spot: a state that discounts loss of control sees a different payoff table.

Whether conditional lab commitments help or hurt. This one is the map's own question; no fetched source argues it. A clause that relaxes safeguards when a rival is reckless can be read as honesty about a real constraint or as a route by which the least careful developer sets everyone's standard. The documents themselves acknowledge the second risk; that is the phrase "race to the bottom" in both.

Scenario writing explores the same ground. AI 2027 (Kokotajlo, Alexander, Larsen, Lifland and Dean, April 2025) is a scenario, not a study. It has a "race" ending and a "slowdown" ending, which branch after a point where a joint company and government committee weighs evidence of misalignment against a rival two months behind. The authors say neither ending is a recommendation.

What you can say now

  • A race lowers safety even when every participant wants safety, so replacing a reckless actor with a careful one changes little.
  • In the Racing to the Precipice model, risk rises with enmity and, in most cases, with the number of teams, and falls when skill matters more than risk-taking.
  • The same model says more knowledge of rivals' capabilities can raise risk, because a leader who knows its margin is thin cuts more.
  • Anthropic's and OpenAI's voluntary frameworks both make some safeguards depend on what competitors do, and both name a race to the bottom as the thing to avoid.
  • MAIM is a proposal, not a policy: deterrence of a dash for AI dominance by the threat of sabotage, disputed by RAND and others as infeasible and destabilising.
  • "The Manhattan Trap" argues a national race to superintelligence is a trust dilemma, so mutual restraint is stable if each side can see the other keeping it.
