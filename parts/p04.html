THE PROBLEM · PART 04 OF 12Fixes, objections, a speed limit

Replies, and Barak's fake graphs

What Kulveit and co-authors propose doing about gradual disempowerment, what their critics and followers say, and Boaz Barak's November 2026 argument for keeping humans in charge.
SourceKulveit, Douglas, Ammann, Turan, Krueger, Duvenaud, Gradual Disempowerment, section 6 (2025) ↗
SourceBoaz Barak, Human empowerment in a few fake graphs (2026) ↗
TL;DR — The paper's authors offer four kinds of response: measure how much say people still have, limit how much AI gets, build people's say back up, and work out what a good arrangement would even look like. They admit the limits are stopgaps. Critics answer that handing work to a loyal, capable helper is not the same as losing power. Boaz Barak adds a separate argument: people should stay in charge even of well-behaved AI, and that may need a cap on how fast AI improves. He says he does not know how to build that cap.
TL;DR, technical — Section 6 of Kulveit et al. (2025) lists four intervention classes: metrics of human influence per system, limits on AI influence (oversight mandates, taxation, norms), stronger human control (faster democratic processes, understandability requirements, AI delegates) and research on “ecosystem alignment”. The authors concede the limits sacrifice value and fail without international coordination. Cowen (2025) and Bentham's Bulldog and Halstead (2026) dispute the mechanism: no collusion model, and delegation to intent-aligned agents with human-owned capital is not disempowerment. Harms (2026) defends the paper; Sahoo (2026) models one mechanism as coupled ODEs. Barak (2026) argues for a cap on the rate of AI progress, not its level, and rejects the Vulnerable World Hypothesis; he gives no mechanism or number.
TL;DR, ELI5 — Picture a family where the robots do more of the chores every month. Some people say: count how many decisions the family still makes, keep a few jobs for people, and teach the robots to explain themselves. Others say: relax, a good helper doing your chores has not taken your house. One scientist says: the robots can keep getting smarter, just not faster than the family can keep up with. He has not worked out how to set that speed.

Parts 02 and 03 laid out the gradual disempowerment argument and its three case studies. This page asks what follows from it. It covers the authors' own proposals, the strongest published objections, two 2026 follow-ups, and a new essay by Boaz Barak that reaches a similar worry from a different direction and ends somewhere the paper does not: a speed limit.

What the authors propose

Section 6 of the paper frames the job as harder than aligning one model. The thing to keep aligned is a set of societal systems that may keep running smoothly after they stop needing people. The authors sort responses into four groups and say a robust answer needs progress on all four.

GroupWhat it containsThe authors' own caveat
MeasureMetrics per system: an AI share of GDP counted apart from labour and capital, the fraction of major corporate decisions made mainly by AI, the share of widely consumed content made by humans, the complexity of legislation as a proxy for whether people can follow it. Also early-warning signs for feedback loops between systems.There is often no outside reference point for how aligned a system is. Where the tipping points sit is listed as an open research question.
Limit AI influenceRules requiring human oversight of critical decisions, limits on AI autonomy and on AI ownership of assets, progressive taxes on AI-generated revenue, cultural norms that favour human agency.These give up value, which creates an incentive to route around them, and they work poorly unless widely adopted. The paper expects them to be mostly stopgaps.
Strengthen human influenceFaster and more representative democratic processes, requirements that AI outputs stay understandable to people, AI delegates that argue for a person's interests, institutions built to survive human obsolescence, forecasting tools such as conditional prediction markets.More direct influence is not automatically better. The paper doubts that a direct democracy would serve citizens well if culture itself has drifted.
System-wide alignmentResearch the authors call “ecosystem alignment”: how a civilisation of interacting human and AI parts can keep serving human values.They say plainly that nobody yet has a convincing positive picture of the end state.

The authors predict the failure mode of their own second group. A country that holds back AI to protect its citizens' influence may simply be outgrown by countries that do not, so the limits depend on international coordination. That dependency is why this map spends its later parts on race dynamics and international coordination.

The proposals follow from the diagnosis. If institutions serve people partly through implicit alignment, meaning they need human work and cooperation to function, then once that need goes, something explicit has to replace it.

The objections

Checks and balances will hold. Tyler Cowen replied in February 2025, calling it one of the smarter arguments he had seen while remaining far from convinced. His points: cheap copies of capable models suggest a decentralised future with wide human ownership; a lone individual already has almost no bargaining power against a society united against them, yet that unity rarely forms; and the paper gives no model of AIs colluding against people. He expects checks and balances to persist.

When were humans ever in control to begin with?Tyler Cowen, “Gradual Empowerment?”, Marginal Revolution, 4 February 2025

On this map's reading, the collusion point lands beside the paper's mechanism, not on it. The paper's conclusion says the effect needs no deliberate or agentic action by AIs, only people and institutions following local incentives. It also takes up checks and balances directly in its section on mutual reinforcement, arguing that the links between economy, culture and state carry influence in whichever direction it flows and do not themselves protect human interests. Cowen's opening question is harder to dismiss. The paper's first premise is that today's systems are fairly aligned with people, and that premise carries a lot of weight.

Delegation is not disempowerment. The most detailed critique came in September 2026 from Bentham's Bulldog and John Halstead. They read the paper's new claim as: even elites end up permanently disempowered by delegating to competent AI that does what they intend. Their reply is that this does not follow. Someone who hands investment decisions to a better investor, or tax affairs to an accountant whose reasoning they cannot follow, has not lost power in any way worth worrying about. If only humans can own capital, then demand is ultimately human demand. And if competition is what forces the hand-over, then competition is the problem, and the paper does not show AI makes it worse than it already is.

In a nutshell, we argue that the novel claims in the paper are not true and the true claims in it are not novel.Bentham's Bulldog and John Halstead, “A critique of gradual disempowerment”, 17 September 2026

They add two framing complaints. Ordinary people losing out to an AI-equipped ruler is a real risk but an older one, and it belongs under concentration of power (part 05). And the paper never says whether losing control is bad in itself or only bad because of what follows. If only outcomes matter and AI would deliver better ones, the same premises argue for handing over control.

Four reasons to rank it lower. The 80,000 Hours problem profile (Cody Fenwick, April 2025) takes the risk seriously and still lists reasons a reader might not: AI that is safe in other respects may help solve this by default; non-scheming AI may be unlikely to produce an existential catastrophe; there may be no good solutions; and a disempowered humanity might not count as a catastrophe at all. The profile calls itself very uncertain and recommends research before policy.

A defence, and two follow-ups

Max Harms answered the critique the same day. His main point is that the critics describe the wrong scenario. The paper is not about faithful assistants serving individual owners. It is about AI systems loyal to institutions: a firm whose AIs maximise the firm's returns, owned largely by other automated firms, with human shareholders unable to oversee any of it. He also points to the paper's passage on competitive pressure as a direct answer to the claim that the pressure argument is missing. He concedes three things: the paper is loose about what “aligned” means, it sometimes overstates its novelty, and whether agency matters for its own sake is left ambiguous.

Subramanyam Sahoo's “Policy Myopia as a Mechanism of Gradual Disempowerment” (March 2026, accepted at an ICLR 2026 workshop) extends the thesis by proposing a specific route. Institutions that chase visible crises stop funding slow preventive work; the people who could do that work leave; and values written into automated systems at one moment stay fixed while human values move on. He argues that fixes which rely on people contesting decisions fail because they need exactly the capacity that has eroded. His remedy is deliberate inefficiency: protected budgets for prevention, decisions that must be deliberated by people, and firewalls between economic, political and cultural power.

Sahoo formalises this as deterministic coupled ODEs with two rates, capacity erosion from delegation (baseline 0.08 per year) and atrophy from disuse (0.09). In the baseline run institutional capacity crosses an irreversibility threshold in 15 to 20 years; contestability mechanisms push that to 25 to 35 years without changing the endpoint. Across his sensitivity scenarios the range is 8 to 50 years. The appendix lists the limits: smooth dynamics, one country's budget data, literature-based parameters, salience treated as exogenous.

The paper's timelines come from a stylised model whose parameters the author describes as substantially uncertain. They illustrate the mechanism; they are not a forecast.

Karim Abdul-Aal's “Preconditions of Gradual Disempowerment” (EA Forum, August 2026) accepts the model and attacks its starting point from the opposite side to Cowen. Both Kulveit et al. and Sahoo, he argues, assume societies begin with healthy collective reasoning and working institutions. Using United States education, media and governance as examples, he argues the decline is already under way, so AI dependence will take hold faster. The author labels the essay a synthesis, not original empirical research.

Barak's graphs, in words

On 23 November 2026 Boaz Barak, a computer scientist whose profile lists Harvard and OpenAI and marks the opinions as his own, posted “Human empowerment in a few fake graphs”. The essay is built around seven charts that he calls fake graphs; it gives no data or units for them. This map does not have the images, so what follows is reconstructed from the text around them. The AI Safety map carries a one-paragraph summary; this is the longer version.

  1. The view he rejects. AI capability climbs while human intelligence stays flat. Past the crossing point people cannot follow what AI does, so AI ends up in control, and the only open question is whether it is benevolent. Barak notes that some in AI safety would count a handover to a benevolent AI as a good result. He does not.
  2. The right comparison is humanity, not one human. Individual brains have not changed, but writing, markets, democracy and technology have kept raising what humanity can do collectively. So the human line slopes upward. It rises more slowly than the AI line, so by itself this only delays the crossing.
  3. Bend both curves. Humans have learned before to supervise projects too large for any one mind, from particle colliders to large software systems. AI can extend that by making the options legible to human decision-makers, which bends the humanity curve up. Barak thinks this will not be enough on the current path, especially once AI accelerates its own development (recursive self-improvement). So the AI curve has to bend down as well.
  4. Aligned versus misaligned. Dropping the assumption that all AI is well-behaved, he splits total intelligence into the part pointed at aligned goals and the part that is misaligned or misused. The best case is that the first stays well above the second. He describes much of technical safety work as widening that gap.
  5. A fixed doom line. Whether a gap suffices depends on the Vulnerable World Hypothesis. As Barak uses it, the hypothesis says some absolute level of capability is enough to destroy the world, and the misaligned line reaching it ends the game however large the gap. (Bostrom's 2019 paper states it in terms of technological development; Barak applies it to intelligence.)
  6. A rising doom line. He does not accept a fixed line. His evidence is historical: the Mongol armies killed a large share of the world's population with horses and swords, and the Black Death killed about half of Europe and is now treatable with antibiotics. The capability needed to end humanity has gone up as societies became more resilient, and AI put to work on resilience could raise it faster.
  7. All of it together. Humanity's curve bent up, AI's bent down, aligned above misaligned, and a destruction threshold that keeps climbing ahead of the misaligned line.

The detail that matters most is in step three. Barak does not ask for a ceiling on how capable AI may become. He asks for a limit on how fast it gets there, so that human comprehension can keep pace.

What Barak says he does not know

I do not have the answers as to how such mechanisms would be implemented, nor to the way to determine the precise “speed limit” for AI growth.Boaz Barak, “Human empowerment in a few fake graphs”, 23 November 2026

The essay is a statement of direction. It names no mechanism for the limit, no number, and no unit for the vertical axis of any chart. Barak says he suspects the answer combines regulation with technical means, and his closing list adds international cooperation on pacing. He is explicit that the good outcome will not arrive by default.

The levers that exist or have been proposed for pacing are the subject of the second half of this map: compute governance, compute thresholds and international coordination. None of them was designed as a rate limit of the kind Barak describes, and he does not endorse any of them in the essay.

How this connects to gradual disempowerment

Barak does not cite the Kulveit paper. The connection drawn here is this map's reading.

The two agree on the claim that separates structural risk from the rest of AI safety: getting each AI system to behave is not sufficient. The paper says methods for aligning individual systems will not stop the drift. Barak says human autonomy and sovereignty should be preserved however well aligned AI turns out to be. That sentence also answers the question Bentham's Bulldog and Halstead put to the paper. Barak treats human control as worth keeping for its own sake, where the paper left its position open.

They differ on the cause. For Barak the danger is a comprehension gap: AI outruns humanity's ability to understand and steer it. For Kulveit and co-authors the danger is an incentive gap: systems stop needing people, so they stop serving them, even if every decision stays perfectly understandable. A world could close one gap and still fall into the other.

Their remedies overlap in one place and diverge in another. Barak's “use AI to make options legible” is close to the paper's understandability requirements and AI delegates. His speed limit sits in the family of measures the paper files under limiting AI influence and expects to be circumvented when they cost too much. The graphs have no axis for competition, and competition between firms and states is exactly what the paper says erodes such limits. Barak's call for international cooperation points at the same gap without filling it.

Carry this away. The authors, their critics and Barak mostly agree that the hard part is not any single AI system. They disagree about whether human control is the goal or only a means, and nobody in this debate has yet shown a mechanism that keeps people in charge when stepping back is the competitive move.

Where people disagree

Is the baseline healthy? Cowen doubts humans were ever in control; Abdul-Aal thinks institutions are already degraded. They draw opposite conclusions (less worry, more worry) from the same doubt about the paper's first premise.

Is control valuable in itself? Bentham's Bulldog and Halstead say that if preference satisfaction is what counts, handing decisions to wiser AI could be right. Harms and Barak both say agency matters as an end. The 80,000 Hours profile lists “disempowerment might not be a catastrophe” as a live position.

Can limits hold? The paper calls limits on AI influence stopgaps. Sahoo's model has procedural safeguards delaying the outcome without preventing it. Barak bets that a pace limit can be made to work and admits he cannot yet say how. This page covers no replies to Barak's essay: it was posted the day this page was checked, 23 November 2026.

What you can say now

  • The gradual-disempowerment authors propose measuring human influence, limiting AI influence, strengthening human control and researching system-wide alignment, and they expect the limits to be stopgaps without international coordination.
  • Cowen's objection is that decentralisation and the absence of AI collusion make lasting checks and balances the likelier outcome.
  • Bentham's Bulldog and Halstead argue that delegating to loyal, competent AI is not disempowerment; Harms replies that the paper is about AI loyal to institutions, not to people.
  • Sahoo's 2026 paper offers one modelled mechanism (attention to visible crises starving long-term capacity), with timelines that are illustrative only.
  • Barak argues for capping the rate of AI progress, not its level, so that humanity's growing collective capability can keep up, and says he has no mechanism or number for it.
  • Barak rejects a fixed world-destroying threshold: he thinks resilience raises it over time and AI can raise it faster.
