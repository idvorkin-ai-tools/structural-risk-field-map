LEVERS · PART 07 OF 12Chips as the governable input

Compute governance

Why computing hardware is the part of AI that can be counted and controlled, what that could be used for, and what could go wrong, as argued by Girish Sastry, Lennart Heim, Haydn Belfield and sixteen co-authors.
SourceSastry, Heim, Belfield, Anderljung, Brundage et al., Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence (2024) ↗
SourceCentre for the Governance of AI, the paper's own page (2024) ↗
TL;DR — Ideas and data can be copied for free, so rules about them are hard to enforce. The specialised chips that train the biggest AI models are physical, expensive, few, and made by a handful of companies. A 2024 paper argues that this makes chips the best place to steer AI from: you can see who has them, decide who gets them, and enforce rules through them. The same paper warns that this handle can be used badly and that it weakens every year as AI gets cheaper to build.
TL;DR, technical — Sastry et al. (arXiv 2402.08797, February 2024) argue that AI compute is the most regulable input to frontier AI because it is detectable, excludable and quantifiable and comes from a highly concentrated supply chain. They sort what it enables into three capacities, visibility, allocation and enforcement, with four illustrative mechanisms each, ranging from reporting rules that existed in 2024 to on-chip enforcement they call speculative and unproven. They list the costs (privacy, leaks, economic drag, centralised power), the erosion from algorithmic and hardware progress and low-compute models, and five guardrails.
TL;DR, ELI5 — Anyone can copy a recipe, so you cannot control recipes. But the very biggest cakes need giant ovens, and only a few factories make giant ovens. So if grown-ups want to know who is baking giant cakes, they count the ovens. The catch: whoever counts the ovens gets a lot of power, and every year people learn to bake bigger cakes in smaller ovens.

Parts 01 to 06 described risks that come from the shape of the situation: no single culprit, many actors each responding to pressure. A structural problem needs a lever that acts on the situation, not on one actor's intentions. Compute governance is a proposal for such a lever: act on the hardware every frontier developer needs. The paper explained here sets out the case and devotes a full section to the downsides, so this page spends as long on the warnings as on the promise.

Why the authors start with compute

The paper treats AI capability as the product of three technical inputs, data, algorithms and computing power, plus the people who combine them. Its claim is narrow: of these, compute is the one a regulator can get a grip on, and it matters most for exactly the large general models that worry people.

On importance, the paper's evidence is the growth curve. Using Epoch's data, it reports that the compute used to train notable models doubled about every six months from 2010, a factor of roughly 350 million in 13 years. Chip price-performance doubled only every two to two and a half years, so most of the growth came from spending more on bigger clusters. Developers paid because scale kept buying capability. Training compute is counted in total operations, usually FLOP.

Two caveats come with this from the start. The paper says compute governance is one tool among several, and that it does not reach small, specialised systems. And its front page states that the nineteen authors do not all agree with every claim.

The authors vary significantly in their views of which of these, if any, would be desirable.
Sastry et al., section 1, on their own list of mechanisms

Four properties that make chips governable

The core of the argument is a comparison. Data, algorithms and trained models are information: once out, they can be copied at no cost, and many people can use the same copy at once. Chips are objects. The paper names four properties that follow, and scores every input against them in its Figure 9; compute is the only one rated high on all four.

PropertyWhat it meansThe paper's evidenceThe paper's own caveat
DetectableLarge-scale AI work is hard to hide.An AI supercomputer is tens of thousands of chips drawing up to dozens of megawatts, in a building that can cost billions and needs permits and a power allocation. The paper cites an estimate of about 140 data centres above 100 MW in 2023.Seeing a building does not tell you what runs inside it. Most large data centres host ordinary workloads too.
ExcludableWhoever holds a chip decides who uses it.You either own the chip or rent time on it, and the holder can cut you off. Two users cannot use the same chip-hour. A published paper or leaked file has neither limit.Intruders can break in and use someone else's compute, though the paper says they are easily expelled once detected.
QuantifiableCompute can be measured and checked.Chips can be counted and have specifications: operations per second, memory, bandwidth between chips. Contrast talent, which resists measurement and is rightly protected by civil liberties.Ways of measuring the compute used in a training run were not yet fully standardised.
Concentrated supply chainA few firms control key steps.In the paper's Figure 11: one firm (ASML) makes all extreme-ultraviolet lithography machines; one foundry (TSMC) made about 90% of logic chips at 7 nm and below in 2022; one designer (NVIDIA) had 80 to 95% of AI chip design in 2023. The lithography machines took decades and billions of dollars to develop.These are facts about the market in 2022 to 2023, and they can change. The same figure notes a Chinese foundry producing 7 nm chips in 2023 with older equipment.

The paper deliberately measures hardware in "operations per second" instead of floating-point operations per second, because training increasingly uses lower-precision arithmetic. It also separates two uses of quantification: measuring a cluster (what could this hardware train?) and measuring a model (how much compute went into it?). The second is what a compute threshold uses; see part 09.

The fourth property strengthens the other three. If every advanced chip passes through two or three companies, then counting, tracking and withholding chips needs the cooperation of very few parties. The paper's Appendix A compares this to uranium enrichment, a physical bottleneck that nuclear governance has leaned on for decades, and adds that the analogy breaks in one important place: nothing in the nuclear case resembles publishing model weights, which lets anyone skip the expensive step.

Why govern development and not only use

Most regulation targets harmful use of a finished product. The paper expects AI rules to do that too, and argues it will not be enough for the most severe risks, for two reasons.

First, deployments are hard to find. One copy of a trained model runs on a small amount of hardware, and copies spread through release or theft. Second, harm does not stay where the model is deployed: a pathogen design or a piece of malware can travel far beyond the system that produced it. So the paper wants some rules to apply earlier, while the model is being built, and compute is the input that makes an early check feasible. It restricts this to harms too large to compensate afterwards.

Three capacities, twelve mechanisms

The paper sorts what compute could do for a regulator into three capacities. Visibility is knowing who is building and running advanced AI. Allocation is influencing who gets compute and for what. Enforcement is being able to prevent or respond to rule-breaking. Each gets four examples. The paper calls them illustrative, not recommendations.

The spread in readiness is the thing to notice. The abstract says some ideas were being piloted while others wait on fundamental research. On the hardware-level enforcement ideas the paper is blunt: it calls them speculative, with feasibility and robustness unproven, and adds that any new mechanism built into chips is also a new thing to attack.

There is no mechanism that differentiates “good AI” from “bad AI.”
Sastry et al., section 4.C, footnote 114

The footnote is about enforcement built into hardware. On this map's reading it describes the whole approach. Hardware can count operations, cap cluster sizes and check licences. It cannot judge purpose. Every mechanism in the table works on quantity and access, and something else has to decide what the quantities mean.

One row has moved since publication. The reporting rules came from Executive Order 14110 of October 2023. Executive Order 14148 revoked it on 20 January 2025. Where thresholds stand in law as of November 2026 is the subject of part 09; export controls in practice are part 08.

The paper's own warnings

Section 5 of the paper is a list of ways compute governance can do damage. The authors sum it up this way:

compute governance is a double-edged sword: it can be used to promote widely shared objectives like safety, but it can also be used to infringe on civil liberties, prop up the powerful, and entrench authoritarian regimes.
Sastry et al., section 5

Four costs are named.

  • Privacy. Computation runs through most of modern life. Any scheme that reports on who computes what can expose individuals and trade secrets, and cloud customers already need assurance that their provider is not looking at their work.
  • Leaks. A regulator that collects every lab's compute figures holds a map of the frontier. The paper calls that a uniquely attractive target for commercial and state espionage, and notes that a leak could speed up the spread of capability the scheme was meant to slow.
  • Economic drag. Computing grew fast partly because nobody needed permission to use it. Checks triggered at a compute threshold that looks high today cover more of the industry as compute use grows.
  • Centralised power. A body that can see, allocate or switch off large compute controls a resource the whole economy increasingly depends on. Firms may try to capture it. A remote off-switch is a security hole. The paper cites history on what centralised authority does to dissent, inequality and decision quality.

On this map's reading, the last item matters most for a map of structural risk. Part 05 covers concentration of power as a harm in its own right. Compute governance works by exploiting concentration (few chip makers, few clouds), and its stronger forms add more (one reserve, one megaproject, a short list of veto-holders). The paper says so directly about its own most ambitious ideas: a "CERN for AI" could be one of the largest expansions of international organisations' power in history and, in the worst case, could hand the technology to an illegitimate government.

There is also a link to part 06. The paper argues that verified information about compute could calm race dynamics, citing the Cold War "missile gap", when wrong estimates of Soviet capability created pressure for an American build-up. It also notes work showing that more information can, in specific cases, make a race worse. How verification would work between states is part 12.

What erodes the lever

The second half of section 5 asks whether the lever keeps working. The paper says compute governance is most effective when a given capability needs a lot of compute, compute is expensive, and large amounts of it must sit in one big data centre. All three conditions are weakening.

Efficiency. Better algorithms cut the operations needed for a given level of performance each year, and better hardware cuts the price of each operation. The paper's answer is to separate two things. The compute needed to match a fixed capability falls. The compute needed to be at the frontier keeps rising as long as scale pays. So compute rules can keep hold of the frontier while yesterday's frontier leaks out to everyone. The authors say this holds only while scaling keeps working and training cannot be split across many small sites.

Small dangerous models. A specialised model can be potent with little compute. The paper's example is AlphaFold 2, trained with under 1023 operations, about a hundredth of what GPT-4-class models used. If the dangerous systems are small, compute rules either miss them or sweep in harmless work.

Cheap changes after training. Running a model costs far less than training it, and so does altering it. The paper cites researchers who removed the safety behaviour of a published open-weights chat model by fine-tuning it for about $200 on one GPU.

Evasion and decoupling. Restricted actors smuggle chips, use chips the controls do not cover, or rent cloud capacity. Over a longer period, cutting a country off gives it a stronger reason to build a supply chain that needs none of the controlled suppliers, which would remove the concentration the lever depends on.

The paper's research appendix offers a view, and asks how far it is correct: compute governance may decide when a capability spreads and to whom, not whether it does. If so, what it buys is time, and the time is worth something only if it is spent preparing.

Five guardrails

The paper closes with five principles for anyone designing compute rules.

  1. Leave small-scale and non-AI compute alone. The paper estimates that high-end data-centre AI chips were fewer than 1 in 400,000 of all chips made in 2022, and that the compute behind the 2023 United States reporting line would have cost at least $100 million to rent. Rules can sit at that scale and never touch a consumer or a university lab.
  2. Use privacy-preserving practices and technology. Collect the minimum, secure it, and invest in methods that prove compliance without exposing the underlying work.
  3. Use compute controls only where acting in advance is justified. Controls on inputs are blunt. Keep them for catastrophic and national-security harms that no fine could repair afterwards.
  4. Revisit thresholds and controlled technology often. The paper suggests at least yearly, because a fixed line goes stale in both directions.
  5. Build in substantive and procedural safeguards. Limit in statute what regulators may collect and control, and add review: public comment, whistleblower protection, courts, published reports.

A sixth point comes from the conclusion. If rules are limited to AI chips, more and more capability will become reachable on hardware nobody governs. The paper's answer is to use the powerful, governable compute to build defences, for example in cybersecurity and biosecurity, before that happens.

Carry this away. Chips are the one input to frontier AI that is physical, scarce and made by a few firms, so they are where a regulator can see, steer and enforce. That handle measures quantity, not intent; it weakens as AI gets cheaper; and whoever holds it holds a great deal of power. The paper that makes the case for the lever also makes the case for keeping it narrow.

Where people disagree

Regulate use, not development. Jeremy Howard's essay "AI Safety and the Age of Dislightenment" (July 2023), which the paper cites, was written against an earlier proposal to license frontier models. It argues that licensing and surveillance of model development would be ineffective or counterproductive. His concern is the one in the warnings above, taken further: if only a few licensed organisations may hold full models, they gain a lasting advantage over everyone else, including the governments meant to oversee them. He would put liability and disclosure duties on risky applications and keep model-building open. The paper's reply is the argument in the upstream section: some harms cannot be caught at the point of use.

The lever will not hold. Howard also argues that limits on training compute cannot be enforced, because people can pool ordinary hardware over the internet, and that making them enforceable would mean monitoring personal computers. The paper, seven months later, calls massively decentralised training technically infeasible at the time of writing and agrees that if it became practical, governments would need other tools. The two disagree about a technical trend, and neither settles it.

It cuts against what governments are doing. Adam Thierer's R Street study "Existential Risks and Global Governance Issues Around AI and Robotics" (June 2023), also cited by the paper, doubts that compute can be meaningfully controlled. He points out that governments were spending heavily to expand computing capacity while others called for limits, says the chip export controls could prove hard to enforce and would be evaded once other sellers emerged, and rejects global bans and mass surveillance as unworkable. He allows narrow, targeted export controls a role and otherwise prefers decentralised, voluntary governance.

Timing and side effects of denial. The paper itself reports, without resolving them, objections to the export controls of 2022 and 2023: that they hurt the controlling countries' own chip firms, that they may have pushed the target toward self-sufficiency sooner, and that enforcement was in doubt. Part 08 follows what happened next.

Compute is a weak proxy. The paper concedes that training compute is only a rough stand-in for capability and expects direct capability measurements to do better over time. Whether a compute line is still a useful first filter is the argument of part 09.

What you can say now

  • Compute is the governable input to AI because it is detectable, excludable, quantifiable and made by a concentrated supply chain; data, algorithms and trained models are none of these to the same degree.
  • Sastry et al. sort the uses into visibility, allocation and enforcement, and say plainly that their twelve mechanisms are illustrations the authors do not all endorse.
  • Readiness varies a lot: reporting and export controls existed in 2024, a chip registry was a proposal, and the paper calls on-chip enforcement speculative and unproven.
  • On this map's reading, hardware can count and cap but cannot tell good AI from bad, so compute rules need something else to give the numbers meaning.
  • The lever erodes as algorithms and chips improve, misses small specialised models, and invites smuggling and a separate supply chain; the paper suggests it may mainly buy time.
  • Compute governance is itself a way to concentrate power, which is why the paper asks for narrow scope, privacy protection, regular review and legal safeguards.
