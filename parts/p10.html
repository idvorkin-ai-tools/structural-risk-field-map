Safety frameworks and the EU code

How a company's self-written safety plan became something a regulator can check: the Seoul commitments, METR's survey of the published frameworks, the EU's Code of Practice and California's SB 53.
SourceEuropean Commission, The General-Purpose AI Code of Practice (published July 2025) ↗
SourceMETR, Common Elements of Frontier AI Safety Policies (December 2025 version) ↗
TL;DR — The biggest AI companies write their own safety plans: which dangerous abilities they test for and what they will do if a model has them. In 2024 that was a voluntary promise. Since then California has turned it into a duty: write the plan, publish it, follow it, and pay a fine if you do not. The EU requires the largest developers to assess and reduce risks, and offers a voluntary code built around such a plan as one accepted way to show they have. The law still does not say where the danger lines sit. The company draws them, and it can redraw them.
TL;DR, technical — The Frontier AI Safety Commitments (Seoul, May 2024) had companies promise to publish a framework with risk thresholds and a stop condition. METR's December 2025 survey finds twelve published policies sharing nine elements. EU AI Act Article 55 imposes evaluation, risk mitigation, incident reporting and cybersecurity duties on providers of general-purpose models with systemic risk; the Safety and Security chapter of the voluntary Code of Practice is one accepted way to show compliance, built on a Framework and a per-model Model Report sent to the AI Office. California SB 53 requires large frontier developers to publish a framework and makes failure to follow it a civil-penalty violation. Thresholds and risk acceptance stay self-set in all three.
TL;DR, ELI5 — Imagine each fireworks factory writes its own safety rulebook. At first the factories only promised to write one. Now one town says: you must write it, you must show it to us, and if you break your own rules we fine you. But each factory still decides what its rules say, and it is allowed to rewrite them.

Parts 07 to 09 were about hardware: who can get chips, and how much computing makes a model count as "frontier". This part is about what a developer owes once it is over that line. The answer that governments have reached so far is unusual. They have not written safety rules for frontier models. They have told developers to write their own, and then attached consequences to them. That design choice matters for structural risk, because it decides how much of the pressure described in Race dynamics the rules can actually resist.

As of November 2026. Voluntary: the Seoul Frontier AI Safety Commitments (2024), with 20 organisations listed on the UK government page. In force, EU: Article 55 of the AI Act has applied since 2 August 2025. The Commission says it enforces the general-purpose model rules with fines from 2 August 2026, and that models placed on the market before 2 August 2025 have until 2 August 2027. This page treats frameworks as a governance instrument. For one lab's thresholds against another's, see the preparedness reports comparison and the AI Safety field map's page on responsible scaling.

What a framework is

A frontier safety framework is a document a developer writes about itself. It names the abilities it considers dangerous, says how it will test for them, and says what protections must be in place before a model with those abilities is trained further or released. The core move is conditional: if the model can do X, then we must have safeguard Y. The level of ability that triggers the "then" is a capability threshold.

METR, the evaluation non-profit, says it introduced the concept in 2023 and that the first such policy was piloted that September. The December 2025 version of its Common Elements report counts twelve companies with a published policy and sorts the contents into nine elements.

Every policy has an update clause and an accountability clause, so every one is explicitly a living document. The element that gives a framework teeth is a stated condition under which the company stops: a condition for halting development is missing from a third of them (8 of 12 have one), and one for halting deployment from a quarter (9 of 12). METR also notes that the policies differ: some emphasise domain-specific risks and not only catastrophic ones, and a few lean on quantitative benchmarks where most do not.

The security element concerns model weights: the policies tie stronger protection against theft to higher capability levels. The elicitation element exists because a test that under-elicits a model reports a capability below the true one, which makes every downstream threshold decision wrong in the unsafe direction.

Seoul, 2024: the voluntary version

At the AI Seoul Summit in May 2024, the UK and Korean governments announced that a group of companies had agreed to the Frontier AI Safety Commitments. METR gives the count as sixteen at the summit and four since; the government page lists twenty. The text calls the commitments voluntary, and asks each company to publish a safety framework focused on severe risks before the next summit, in France.

There are eight commitments under three outcomes: manage risk, be accountable, be transparent. In short, each company is to assess risk before deployment and where appropriate during training, set thresholds at which a risk would be intolerable unless mitigated, say how mitigations keep risk under them and what it will do if one is met, build internal governance, and publish how it is doing all this and how outsiders are involved. The fourth commitment contains the sentence that makes this more than a testing pledge:

In the extreme, organisations commit not to develop or deploy a model or system at all, if mitigations cannot be applied to keep risks below the thresholds.
Frontier AI Safety Commitments, item IV

Three features set the pattern for what followed. The company sets its own thresholds, with input from "trusted actors" such as its home government. The transparency commitment has a carve-out for information that would raise risk or reveal sensitive commercial detail. And the text says a company's approach may change, in which case it will explain why publicly. Nothing in the document says what happens if a company does not do these things.

The EU: a duty, and a code that shows how to meet it

The EU AI Act puts extra duties on providers of what it calls general-purpose AI models with systemic risk. Article 55 is short. Those providers must evaluate the model with state-of-the-art methods, including adversarial testing; assess and mitigate systemic risks; track serious incidents and report them to the AI Office without undue delay; and keep the model and its physical infrastructure adequately protected against cyber attack. Which models fall in this class is a compute threshold question and belongs to part 09.

Article 55 does not say how. Its second paragraph lets providers rely on a code of practice to demonstrate compliance until a harmonised standard exists; a provider who follows neither must show the Commission some other adequate means. The GPAI Code of Practice is that code. The Commission's page says it was written by independent experts, published on 10 July 2025, and confirmed by the Commission and the AI Board as an adequate voluntary tool. Of its three chapters, Transparency and Copyright are for every general-purpose model provider. Safety and Security is for the few with systemic-risk models.

The Safety and Security chapter has ten commitments. On the text at code-of-practice.ai (an unofficial copy; the official PDFs are linked from the Commission page), they come to two documents and a loop.

  • The Framework (commitment 1). The company's standing plan. It must describe and justify the company's "systemic risk tiers": measurable, defined by model capabilities, with at least one tier its models have not yet reached. It must say what mitigations each tier requires and forecast when the company expects to pass its current highest tier. The company reassesses it at least every 12 months, keeps a changelog, and gives the AI Office unredacted access within five business days of confirming or changing it.
  • The Model Report (commitment 7). One per model, to the AI Office by the time the model goes on the market. It must justify in detail why the risks are acceptable, state the safety margin, and name the foreseeable conditions under which that justification would stop holding. It attaches reports from independent external evaluators, or justifies their absence.
  • The loop (commitments 2 to 6). Identify the systemic risks, analyse them, judge them against the company's own acceptance criteria, and if they fail, mitigate and go round again. Measure 4.2 says the signatory proceeds only if the risks are determined acceptable; otherwise its options include not releasing, restricting, withdrawing or recalling the model.
  • Around it (commitments 8 to 10). Named responsibility inside the company, record keeping, and serious-incident reports to the AI Office on fixed clocks: for example, an initial report within two days for a serious disruption of critical infrastructure.

Appendix 1.4 fixes a minimum list of risks every signatory must consider: chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear (CBRN) risks, loss of control, cyber offence, and harmful manipulation.

The fireworks factory keeps two papers. One is the rulebook: "if we ever make a rocket this big, we need a wall this thick." The other is a note for each new rocket: "here is why this one is safe to sell, and here is what would change our mind." The town inspector gets a copy of both.

What signing does, and who signed

Signing creates no new legal obligations. The Commission's Q&A on the code says it "does not impose obligations" and only helps providers meet those the Act already sets. Its Q&A on general-purpose models is precise about the legal effect: adherence is a means of demonstrating compliance, not a presumption of conformity. Commitments made under a code can also count as a mitigating factor when a fine is set.

What a signatory gets is a different kind of supervision. The Q&A on signing says enforcement for signatories will focus on whether they adhere to the code. A provider who does not sign must explain how its own measures comply, for instance through a gap analysis against the code, and may face more requests for information and for access to evaluate its models. A signatory can withdraw at any time.

The Commission's list, last updated 7 October 2026, has 22 signatories, among them Amazon, Anthropic, Google, IBM, Microsoft, Mistral AI and OpenAI. A separate note says xAI signed only the Safety and Security chapter, so it must show compliance with the transparency and copyright duties by other adequate means. A developer absent from the list is still bound by Article 55 if its models qualify; it has only declined this route to showing compliance.

The start was staged. Under Article 113, the chapter on general-purpose models applied from 2 August 2025, with the fining article held back. The Commission's Q&A says the AI Office would spend the first year working with signatories in good faith, and that from 2 August 2026 it enforces with fines. Article 101 caps those at 3% of worldwide annual turnover or EUR 15 million, whichever is higher. This page did not find, and so does not report, any decision using those powers.

California SB 53: publish it, then follow it

California's Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, SB 53, was signed on 29 September 2025; the Future of Privacy Forum's comparison gives 1 January 2026 as its operative date. Its heaviest duties fall on a "large frontier developer": one that has trained a model above the statute's compute line and, with affiliates, had more than $500 million in gross revenue in the previous year.

Such a developer must write, implement, comply with, and publish on its website a "frontier AI framework". The statute lists what it must address, including how the developer defines and assesses its thresholds, what mitigations follow, how it uses third parties, how it secures unreleased weights, and how it manages risk from using its own models internally. Any material change must be published with a justification within 30 days.

With each new frontier model, a large developer must also publish a transparency report that summarises the catastrophic-risk assessments done under its framework, their results, and how far third-party evaluators were involved. Publishing this inside a larger document such as a system card counts. Critical safety incidents go to the California Office of Emergency Services within 15 days.

Section 22757.15 is what makes the framework more than a publication. It lists the violations that carry a civil penalty of up to $1 million each, recoverable only by the Attorney General: failing to publish a required document, making a materially false or misleading statement, failing to report an incident, and this:

fails to comply with its own frontier AI framework
California SB 53, section 22757.15(a)

The statute allows redactions for trade secrets, cybersecurity, public safety and national security, provided the developer describes what it removed and why.

On this map's reading, all three share one design. The state does not decide what is too dangerous. It requires the developer to decide, write the decision down and hand it over, and then holds the developer to what was written. The move from 2024 to 2026 is from a promise nobody could enforce to the same promise with a reader and a penalty attached.

The two legal versions differ in who the reader is. The EU route puts the full framework and model report in front of a regulator that can evaluate the model itself. The California route puts the framework in front of everyone, with redactions, and lets the state punish gaps between words and conduct. Miles Kodama, who annotates the statute at sb53.info, calls public publication a key respect in which SB 53 is stronger than the code.

Carry this away. Current law does not tell a frontier developer where the red lines are. It tells the developer to draw them, show them, and keep to them. That makes lying and quietly ignoring your own plan costly. It does not make a weak plan illegal.

Limits of the design

Self-set thresholds. In the code, the signatory defines and justifies its own risk tiers and acceptance criteria. In SB 53, the framework must describe how the developer defines thresholds, and the statute sets no minimum for where they sit. A developer that sets permissive thresholds and follows them has, on the text, complied with its own framework.

Self-assessment. The developer also runs most of the tests that decide whether a threshold was crossed. Anthropic, in the post announcing version 3 of its framework, describes a "zone of ambiguity": models that pass the quick tests, so low risk can no longer be argued, without evidence strong enough to show high risk. The International AI Safety Report 2026 adds that outside assessments of compliance remain limited, partly because there are no standardised external audits. Outside testing, including external red teaming, appears in the code and SB 53 as something to describe or justify, not as a fixed requirement; the Seoul text affirms it as a best practice to implement without saying how much.

Revision. Every instrument here expects the framework to change and asks only that the change be logged and explained. Anthropic's policy page shows the pace: a comprehensive rewrite in February 2026 and four further versions by July, two of which revise a capability threshold, each published with reasons. On this map's reading, "comply with your own framework" binds a developer to the current version, and the developer issues the versions.

Competitors. A framework is one firm's promise in a market with race dynamics. The same Anthropic post separates what the company will do regardless of others from what it thinks the whole industry would need to do, and explains why:

the robust mitigations we laid out in the prior RSP might prove outright impossible to implement without collective action
Anthropic, Responsible Scaling Policy Version 3.0 (February 2026)

That is the argument of part 06 stated by a participant. A law that makes every large developer publish and follow a framework narrows the gap between careful and careless firms. It does not close it, because each firm still writes its own.

Scope. These instruments are built around severe, identifiable events. SB 53 defines catastrophic risk by a single incident contributing to more than 50 deaths or serious injuries, or more than $1 billion in damage. The code's required list is four risk types. On this map's reading, the slow harms of parts 01 to 05, which have no single incident, mostly fall outside what a framework is asked to measure.

Where people disagree

Is this regulation or disclosure? The International AI Safety Report 2026 calls frameworks the most detailed form of voluntary organisational risk management in use, and in the same passage says they vary substantially in scope, thresholds and enforceability and may not, on their own, ensure effective risk management. It adds that it is too early to assess the new legal requirements. Supporters of the transparency approach can point to the Anthropic post, which credits voluntary frameworks with informing SB 53, New York's RAISE Act and the EU code.

Is the code aimed at the right firms? The chairs and vice-chairs who drafted the Safety and Security chapter published a statement with it. They wrote the chapter assuming about 5 to 15 providers would be covered, warned that the Act's compute line may soon catch far more models, and asked the Commission to update it. They also named whistleblower protection as a remaining legal gap.

Are the penalties large enough to matter? Kodama's annotation observes that a $1 million maximum is small next to the revenue of the companies it applies to, and that the EU ceiling is higher. On this map's reading, the counter-argument is that the penalty attaches to false statements and departures from a published plan, so the exposure is wider than the fine. This page found no enforcement record to test either view.

Do goals constrain as commitments do? Anthropic describes parts of its current approach as "nonbinding but publicly-declared" targets that it will grade itself against. One view is that honest goals beat commitments a firm cannot keep alone. The other is that goals give a regulator less to hold the firm to. The sources here do not settle it.

What you can say now

  • A frontier safety framework is a developer's own if-then plan: named dangerous capabilities, tests for them, and the safeguards required before going further.
  • The Seoul commitments of 2024 were voluntary and had no stated consequence; they did include a promise not to build or release a model whose risks could not be kept under the company's thresholds.
  • In the EU the binding duty is Article 55; the Code of Practice is a voluntary way to show compliance, it is not a presumption of conformity, and the Commission says it enforces with fines from 2 August 2026.
  • California SB 53 makes large frontier developers publish a framework and makes failing to follow it a violation, with penalties up to $1 million each, brought only by the Attorney General.
  • In all three, the company sets the thresholds, runs most of the tests, and may revise the document, so the law checks honesty and consistency more than it checks adequacy.
  • A framework is one firm's promise; whether it holds under competition is the race problem, and the published record includes one developer saying its strongest mitigations need collective action.
