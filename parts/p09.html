LEVERS · PART 09 OF 12Which models count as frontier

Compute thresholds

What a training-compute threshold is for, in Lennart Heim and Leonie Koessler's account, and the thresholds that legislators and regulators have written down, each read from its own text.
SourceHeim & Koessler, Training Compute Thresholds: Features and Functions in AI Regulation (2024) ↗
SourceEU AI Act, Article 51: classification of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) ↗
TL;DR — A rule about "frontier AI" has to say which models it means. Most rules answer with a number: how much computing went into training the model. The EU draws its line at 1025 operations; California, New York and a bill in the US Congress draw theirs at 1026, and add a test of how much money the developer makes. The number does not measure danger. It is a cheap way to decide whom to look at more closely, and it goes stale as methods improve.
TL;DR, technical — Heim & Koessler (arXiv 2405.10799) argue that training compute is the best available metric for an initial regulatory filter because it tracks capability, is cheap to measure, hard to game, knowable before training, externally verifiable and proportional to cost, and that it should trigger oversight and evaluation, not fix mitigations. In law as of November 2026: EU AI Act Article 51(2) presumes systemic risk above 1025 FLOP (rebuttable under Article 52); California SB 53 and New York's amended RAISE Act define a frontier model as above 1026 operations, counting fine-tuning, with a $500 million revenue test for "large frontier developer"; H.R. 9925 (the FRONTIER Act) proposes the same compute line with spending and revenue tiers; Executive Order 14110's 1026 reporting trigger was revoked in January 2025. Algorithmic efficiency, distillation and inference-time scaling all weaken the proxy.
TL;DR, ELI5 — On a motorway, lorries above a certain weight have to pull into the weigh station and get checked. A heavy lorry is not a dangerous lorry. Weight is just easy to read off a scale, and the heavy ones are the ones worth a look. AI rules do the same with how much computer work it took to build a model: over the line, you get checked. The trouble is that builders keep learning to make strong models that weigh less.

Parts 07 and 08 treated computing power as something a state can see and restrict. This page is about a quieter use of the same quantity: as the definition that decides which models, and so which companies, the other levers apply to. Every duty in parts 10 and 11 starts with someone being over a line. Where that line sits, and what is counted towards it, is a design choice with consequences, and it is written into several laws in slightly different ways.

As of November 2026. In force: the EU AI Act's 1025 FLOP presumption (Article 51, applicable since 2 August 2025). Enacted: California's SB 53 definitions (signed 29 September 2025; the Future of Privacy Forum's comparison gives 1 January 2026 as the operative date, which this page did not confirm from the official text). Enacted, not yet effective: New York's RAISE Act as rewritten by Chapter 96 of 2026, which sets 1 January 2027 as its effective date. Proposed: the federal FRONTIER Act, H.R. 9925, introduced 23 July 2026 and referred to committee; it is a bill, not law. Withdrawn: Executive Order 14110 and its 1026 reporting trigger, revoked on 20 January 2025; and the export-control licence requirement for model weights above 1026 operations, covered in part 08.

What is being counted

Training a model means running a very large number of arithmetic steps. A compute threshold is a line drawn on the total count of those steps. The usual unit is the floating-point operation, or FLOP. It is a total, not a speed: a chip has a speed in operations per second, and a training run has a total equal to speed times time times the number of chips.

Heim and Koessler give a worked figure. One NVIDIA A100 running for a week performs about 1.9 × 1020 operations. By this page's arithmetic on that figure, 1025 operations is about 53,000 A100-weeks, and 1026 is ten times that. The paper also translates the totals into money using 2024 rental prices: roughly $7 million of compute for a 1025 run and $70 million for 1026, for the final training run only and not counting staff or experiments.

The frontier does not sit still. The paper reports that the compute used for the largest training runs has grown about fourfold a year since 2010. A fixed line is therefore crossed by more models every year.

A filter, not a verdict

The paper's central claim is about function. A compute threshold should decide who gets looked at. It should not decide what they must then do.

The authors list six properties that make training compute suitable for that first job:

  • It tracks risk, loosely. More compute tends to mean lower loss, lower loss tends to mean more capability, and more capable models can do more damage and get used more widely.
  • It is easy to measure. One number, computable from the training set-up or from hardware usage.
  • It is hard to dodge. With a given method, using less compute gives you a weaker model. A developer cannot slip under the line and keep the capability.
  • It is known early. Developers budget compute before a run starts, so duties can attach before the model exists.
  • Outsiders can check it. A cloud provider can see how much compute a customer used without seeing the model.
  • It tracks cost. A high line touches only organisations that can afford very large runs and leaves start-ups and universities alone.

Against these the paper sets one large weakness, and states it bluntly:

compute is only a crude proxy for model capabilities and an even cruder proxy for risks of large-scale societal harmHeim & Koessler, executive summary

The chain from compute to loss to capability to harm has three links and none is tight. Harm also depends on things no property of the model captures: who wants to misuse it, and how well society can absorb a failure.

So the paper proposes two stages. Compute sorts all models into a large group that can be ignored and a small group that deserves attention. For the small group, slower and more informative tools take over: evaluations, risk assessments, and a capability threshold that decides which safeguards are needed. The authors add that a model which passes the compute line and then tests as harmless should be allowed to drop the precautions.

They point out that this pattern is ordinary regulation. The EU's Digital Services Act gives extra duties to platforms with 45 million monthly users in the Union. User count is not a measure of harm either. It is a cheap sign of where harm at scale could occur.

The paper is explicit that capability evaluations cannot do the filtering job themselves. They cannot be run before training, they need access to the model, results depend on prompting and elicitation, the absence of a capability is hard to prove, and a developer or a model could underperform on purpose (sandbagging). It also argues against "effective compute" as a legal metric for now, because it must be normalised to some performance measure and no standard one exists.

The executive order. Executive Order 14110 told the Secretary of Commerce to require developers of the largest models to report their training plans, how they protect the model weights, and the results of red-team tests. The 1026 figure was a stopgap, to apply until Commerce set technical conditions of its own. It was a reporting trigger and nothing more. Executive Order 14148 revoked the whole order on 20 January 2025. The number outlived it: every later American text in the table uses 1026.

The EU. Article 51 works by presumption. A general-purpose model trained with more than 1025 FLOP is presumed to have high-impact capabilities, which makes it a general-purpose AI model with systemic risk. Under Article 52 the provider must tell the Commission within two weeks of meeting the condition, or of learning that it will. The provider may argue that this particular model, despite its compute, poses no systemic risk, and the Commission decides. The line is also not the only door: the Commission can designate a model by decision, having regard to criteria listed in Annex XIII of the Act. This is close to the two-stage design Heim and Koessler recommend, and they say so.

The Commission uses a second, much lower compute figure for a different question. Its guidelines for providers treat a model trained with more than 1023 FLOP that can generate language, images from text or video from text as, by default, a general-purpose model at all. That is guidance, labelled as an indicative criterion, not a line in the Act.

California. SB 53's definitions build two tiers. A "frontier developer" is anyone who has trained, or started training, a model above 1026 operations. A "large frontier developer" is one of those whose group revenue topped $500 million in the previous calendar year. Some duties, such as reporting critical safety incidents, fall on every frontier developer. The duty to write and publish a safety framework falls only on the large ones. The compute line picks out the model; the revenue line picks out the firm.

New York. The RAISE Act has two versions and the difference is instructive. The text signed in December 2025 (S. 6953-B) defined a frontier model by compute and cost together: more than 1026 operations with a compute cost above $100 million. It also covered a model made by distillation from a frontier model if that cost more than $5 million, and it defined a "large developer" by $100 million of total compute spending. The chapter amendment (S. 8828, signed 27 March 2026) repealed that article and replaced it with definitions that match California's nearly word for word. The cost test and the distillation clause are gone.

The federal bill. H.R. 9925 copies the 1026 definition and changes the second test. It measures the developer over a rolling 36 months and requires both revenue and heavy spending on AI development, in two tiers. It would let a Commerce official raise the compute and money lines by regulation, and only raise them. It also contains a clause that would preempt some state requirements on AI developers. This page confirmed only the introduced version; treat anything about its progress as unknown.

Export control rules have used the same number. The January 2025 diffusion rule required a licence to export the unpublished weights of models trained on more than 1026 operations. The November 2026 remote-access rule revokes that control. Part 08 has both.

The small print: what counts towards the line

Two texts with the same exponent can still cover different models, because they count different things.

Heim and Koessler recommend counting only the main training run. Their reasons: the evidence that links compute to capability is about that run, later fine-tuning usually adds around 1% to the total, and re-measuring after every modification would multiply paperwork and the number of regulated parties.

The laws went the other way. The EU's threshold refers to the "cumulative" amount of computation, and the paper quotes a recital of the Act that lists pre-training, synthetic data generation and fine-tuning as included. California, New York and the federal bill all state that the count includes the original run plus any later fine-tuning, reinforcement learning or other material changes the developer makes to an earlier model.

Why a threshold ages

A compute line is a snapshot of how much compute it took to reach worrying capability with the methods of the day it was drafted. Four things move after that day.

Methods get more efficient. Better architectures and training recipes reach the same capability with fewer operations. To keep the line attached to a fixed capability, you would lower it. But the paper notes the opposing pull: as efficiency and cheaper hardware bring more developers over a given line, the cost of regulating them all rises, which argues for raising it. Heim and Koessler call this a regulatory dilemma and do not pretend it resolves cleanly.

Hardware gets cheaper. This changes who can afford to cross the line. It does not change how capable a model above the line is. The paper criticises Article 51(3) of the EU Act for listing "increased hardware efficiency" beside algorithmic improvement as if the two bore on risk in the same way.

Capability can be copied cheaply. The International AI Safety Report 2026 describes distillation, in which a student model is trained on a stronger model's outputs, as a cheap route to capabilities that were expensive to produce the first time. On this map's reading, that means a distilled model can sit below a compute line that its teacher crossed. New York's first text had a clause for this case; its current text does not.

Compute moves to inference. The same report says that inference-time scaling, where a system spends more computation while answering, lets developers raise capability without more training compute, and that this may weaken thresholds based on training compute. The definitions in the table count compute used to train and modify a model, not compute spent when it answers.

There is also plain drift. When the paper was written in mid-2024, it reported that no model was known to have crossed 1026 and that the American line was aimed at future systems. sb53.info, an unofficial annotated copy of the California law, says that, by Epoch AI's estimates, no published model was above that line until early 2025 and two companies had passed it by the time the annotation was written. This page did not check that estimate against Epoch's data.

The texts differ in how they plan for this. The EU lets the Commission amend the number by delegated act. California asks its Department of Technology to assess the definitions every year, starting by 1 January 2027, and recommend updates; on this map's reading of the section, a recommendation is all it can make. The federal bill allows increases only. Sastry and co-authors, the compute governance paper of part 07, advise reviewing thresholds at least yearly because they can become both too loose and too strict.

Carry this away. A compute threshold answers "whom should we look at?", not "is this model dangerous?". Read any such rule by asking four things: what the line is, what is counted towards it, whether a second test narrows it to large firms, and who has the power to move it. On this map's reading, the revenue and spending tests show what the lines are for in practice: they select a small set of very large developers, which is the same concentration that part 05 treats as a risk, here used as a regulatory convenience.

Where people disagree

Whether the proxy holds at all. Sara Hooker's essay On the Limitations of Compute Thresholds as a Governance Strategy is the fullest critique. She argues that the link between training compute and risk is uncertain and shifting, that smaller models keep getting better through data quality and optimisation, and that the field has a poor record of predicting which abilities appear at which scale. Her summary:

Applying hard coded thresholds to a quickly changing distribution is likely to fail.Hooker, section 1, point 5

She recommends a dynamic threshold set at a percentile of the models released each year, combined with an index of several measures in place of compute alone. She also warns that FLOP is under-specified in the legal texts, which invites Goodhart's law: once the count is a target, developers have reasons to count favourably.

The reply. Heim and Koessler answer Hooker directly. They accept that compute is a crude proxy and say her critique attacks a use the laws do not make of it. A filter only has to be cheap, early and hard to game; the precise work happens afterwards. The alternatives she favours, they argue, cannot be measured before a model exists and are easier to manipulate. They agree that a dynamic threshold tied to the largest model of the day is a real option, but want regulators able to override it.

What a threshold leaves out. Hooker observes that a line set above almost every deployed model directs attention to future systems and away from harms that current models already cause. Heim and Koessler's answer is that most AI rules should not depend on compute at all: bias, privacy, copyright and liability belong to other parts of the law. Sastry and co-authors make a related point from inside compute governance: a compute line used as the sole basis for policy worries them, because a cheap enhancement could change what a small model can do.

What you can say now

  • A compute threshold is a count of training operations used to decide which models get regulatory attention; Heim and Koessler defend it as a first filter and say it should not set the final safeguards.
  • The EU line is 1025 FLOP and works as a rebuttable presumption; the American lines are all 1026, a number that began in an executive order revoked in January 2025.
  • California and New York pair the compute line with a $500 million revenue test, so the heaviest duties land on large firms, not on every model above the line.
  • The federal FRONTIER Act is a bill; it would use the same compute line with revenue and spending tiers, and its lines could only be raised.
  • Thresholds go stale because of algorithmic efficiency, distillation and inference-time compute, and the laws differ in who can update them.
  • What happens once a developer is over the line is a separate question: published frameworks and the EU code in part 10, liability in part 11.
